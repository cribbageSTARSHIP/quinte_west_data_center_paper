### Section IV: The Fiscal Deficit, Subsidy-to-Job Ratios, and Economic Realities

#### 4.1 Tax Incentives vs Infrastructure Costs
Municipalities offering tax breaks, PILOT agreements, and sales tax exemptions to data centers often underestimate the true costs of infrastructure and utility expansion. For instance, Virginia’s 2009 sales tax exemption for data center equipment [[Source: From Energy Use to Air Quality the Many Ways Data Centers Affect US Communities.pdf, Page 2]] has attracted significant investment but shifted the burden of road maintenance, grid upgrades, and water management to local taxpayers. A 2023 study estimated that infrastructure costs for data center clusters exceed $150 million per 100 MW of capacity, far outpacing the $10–$50 million in annual tax incentives offered by states like Virginia and Texas [[Source: Health implications of the rapid rise of data centers in Virginia an exploratory assessment.pdf, Page 2]].

#### 4.2 Subsidy-to-Job Ratios and Labor Spinoffs
Data center development generates temporary construction jobs but few permanent roles. Analysis of 1,200 U.S. facilities found that even the largest data centers employ <150 permanent workers, with salaries ranging from $74,000 (technician) to $160,000 (operations manager) [[Source: From Energy Use to Air Quality the Many Ways Data Centers Affect US Communities.pdf, Page 9]]. Subsidy-per-job ratios in Lancaster, PA, required $10 million in community benefits per data center, yet only 200–300 temporary construction jobs were created during development [[Source: From Energy Use to Air Quality the Many Ways Data Centers Affect US Communities.pdf, Page 12]]. This model produces a public subsidy cost-per-permanent-job of ~$50,000–$100,000, with no local supply-chain multipliers due to the sector’s reliance on outsourced logistics and national tech firms.

#### 4.3 Policy Recommendations for Ontario Municipalities
Ontario municipalities facing hyperscale land assemblies (e.g., Quinte West) should:
1. **Cap tax incentives** at 50% of projected infrastructure costs, requiring developers to fund grid upgrades, road expansions, and water treatment retrofits.
2. **Mandate local supply-chain integration** via zoning requirements: 30% of construction materials must originate from within 100 km, and 15% of permanent staff must reside in the municipality.
3. **Adopt conditional subsidies** tied to clean energy commitments (e.g., 100% renewable power procurement) and noise mitigation plans, as seen in Lancaster’s community benefits agreement.
