# Section 3: Chemical Spills and Runoff in Data Center Infrastructure

## 1. Closed-Loop Cooling Fluid Mechanics and Biochemical Oxygen Demand

Closed-loop cooling systems in data centers commonly employ propylene glycol as a primary component of chiller fluids, owing to its low toxicity and high heat transfer efficiency. However, propylene glycol exhibits a distinct degradation curve under anaerobic conditions, contributing to a Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD) load of 1.68 kg O₂ per liter of solution. This high BOD is exacerbated by secondary additives such as biocides and corrosion inhibitors, which can persist in water systems for extended periods, further altering aquatic oxygen dynamics.

The decomposition of propylene glycol follows a first-order kinetic model under anaerobic bacterial activity, with a half-life of approximately 12–14 days in closed-loop retention systems. However, the presence of biocidal agents, such as quaternary ammonium compounds, can extend this duration by 15–20% by inhibiting microbial activity. This persistence highlights the need for continuous monitoring of chiller fluid leaks, as even minor spillages can generate localized hypoxia in stormwater retention ponds and adjacent ecosystems.^[Source: raw/papers/paper5.md, Page 89]


## 2. Case Study: Cambridge TOR1 Data Center Stormwater Retention Pond Incident

The uncontrolled discharge of an odorous, milky-white substance from the Ascent TOR1 Data Center (17 Vondrau Drive, Cambridge, Ontario) into a stormwater retention pond on July 2026 underscores critical infrastructure gaps in municipal spill response protocols. The incident, attributed to an anaerobic bacterial bloom linked to propylene glycol and biocide leaks, prompted immediate containment efforts by the Ministry of the Environment, Conservation and Parks (MECP) Spills Action Centre, which assumed jurisdiction over private property mitigation. However, the incident exposed significant disparities between private data center infrastructure and municipal stormwater management systems.

The Grand River Conservation Authority (GRCA) conducted hydrodynamic modeling to assess contaminant travel times to the City of Brantford’s drinking water intake, revealing that retention pond outflows could reach municipal water sources within 18–24 hours during high-flow conditions. This highlights the limitations of current retention pond design in isolating chemical spills from downstream municipal water systems. Furthermore, the absence of mandatory isolation shutoff valves in private data center stormwater infrastructure exacerbated the event’s environmental impact, as there were no engineered barriers to prevent direct spillage into the pond.^[Source: CTV News Kitchener | https://www.ctvnews.ca/kitchener/article/crews-cleaning-up-milky-white-substance-from-stormwater-pond-in-cambridge-ont/; CambridgeToday | https://www.cambridgetoday.ca/local-news/spill-cleanup-continues-at-cambridge-data-centre-stormwater-pond-12602383]


## 3. Policy Recommendations for Ontario Municipalities

To mitigate future chemical spill risks, Ontario municipalities—including Quinte West and Waterloo Region—should adopt mandatory isolation shutoff valves in all private data center stormwater systems. These valves would act as a first line of defense against uncontrolled discharges into municipal retention infrastructure. In addition, municipalities should mandate real-time water quality monitoring in retention ponds located within 500 meters of drinking water intakes, with data streamed to local conservation authorities for immediate response.

Provincial legislation should also be revised to require data centers to submit annual spill risk assessments, including detailed retention pond capacity analyses and chemical inventory disclosures. Furthermore, municipalities should establish buffer zones of at least 100 meters between data center stormwater infrastructure and municipal drinking water intakes, as recommended by the Grand River Conservation Authority in the Cambridge TOR1 case. Finally, funding mechanisms should be created to support GRCA-level hydrodynamic modeling for all municipal water systems, ensuring proactive spill mitigation rather than reactive containment.
