# dB(A) and dB(C) in Noise Assessments

Noise assessments measure sound in decibels (dB) using two weighting systems: **dB(A)** and **dB(C)**. These metrics reflect how human hearing perceives sound and regulatory thresholds for safety.

## Key Differences

- **dB(A)**: Simulates human hearing by emphasizing frequencies we perceive more keenly (e.g., high-pitched sounds). Used for **average noise exposure** over time (typically a workday). Legal limits: 80-85 dB(A) for daily exposure.
- **dB(C)**: Measures **peak or impact noise** with equal sensitivity across frequencies. Legal limits: 135-137 dB(C) for instantaneous exposure.

## Why Both Matter

While dB(A) reflects long-term averages (e.g., 88 dB(A) in a warehouse), **dB(C)** captures sudden impacts (e.g., 138 dB(C) from a pallet drop). Both are critical for safety:
- **dB(A)**: Prevents cumulative hearing damage from prolonged exposure.
- **dB(C)**: Mitigates risk from sudden, high-intensity events (e.g., machinery impacts, gunfire).

## Practical Examples

- A warehouse with an average dB(A) of 75 dB(A) may have a peak dB(C) of 138 dB(C) from a dropped pallet.
- Hearing protection effectiveness is calculated using the formula: `Noise under protector = (dB(C) - SNR) + 4dB`.
