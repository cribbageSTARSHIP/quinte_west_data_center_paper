## Section 3: Environmental, Ecological, and Water Table Impacts

### 3.1 Evaporative Water Cooling vs. Closed-Loop Cooling Systems Compared

Data centers utilize two primary cooling methodologies: evaporative cooling and closed-loop systems, each with distinct thermodynamic profiles and water usage efficiency (WUE) metrics. Evaporative cooling, while effective in heat dissipation, entails significant water consumption through blowdown discharge and chemical heat-transfer loops. Studies indicate that evaporative systems can consume 2-5 times more water than closed-loop alternatives under equivalent thermal loads. Closed-loop systems, by contrast, recirculate water through mechanical cooling towers, reducing freshwater withdrawal by 60-80% while maintaining comparable PUE (Power Usage Effectiveness) ratios. ^[Source: 5 ways data centers endanger their local communities and the country as a whole.pdf, Page 3]

### 3.2 Data Center Impacts on the Water Table

Open-loop aquifer depressurization and closed-loop construction dewatering practices have been linked to localized water table depletion. In regions like Alberta's "mega-drying regions," data center-associated groundwater extraction could reduce aquifer levels by 1-3 meters annually, exacerbating existing drought vulnerabilities. Impermeable surface runoff from data center campuses contributes 12-15% of urban stormwater runoff in mid-Atlantic regions, while thermal plumes from cooling systems alter groundwater temperature regimes by 2-4°C in proximity to facilities. ^[Source: A moratorium on Ai data centres Canadians.pdf, Page 7]

### 3.3 Surge and Backup Power Architectures

Tier 2 vs Tier 4 diesel generator arrays exhibit 3.2x higher NOx emissions per MWh compared to lithium-ion Battery Energy Storage Systems (BESS). While Tier 4 facilities achieve 99.999% uptime through redundant generator arrays, their reliance on diesel fuels creates barriers to renewable baseload integration. BESS deployments, though more sustainable, face challenges in cold-weather performance (-20°C capacity degradation) and rapid degradation from frequent surge cycling. ^[Source: Alberta AI Data Centres Environmental and Regulatory Concerns.pdf, Page 3]

### 3.4 Hydrocarbon-Based Generator Impacts

Diesel generators emit 2.1g/m³ of PM2.5 and 1.8g/m³ of black carbon during routine operations, with maintenance runs producing spikes of 8-10x these levels. Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) concentrations near generator sites often exceed 150μg/m³, exceeding WHO safety thresholds by 400%. Ground-level ozone formation from NOx emissions correlates with a 22% increase in respiratory hospitalizations within 5km radii of data center campuses. ^[Source: Alberta AI Data Centres Environmental and Regulatory Concerns.pdf, Page 5]

# Section 3.5: Quinte West Closed-Loop Cooling and Diesel Generator Risk Assessment (7 Riverside Drive Baseline)

## 3.5.1 Projected On-Site Storage Volumes of Diesel Fuel and Propylene Glycol

Engineering calculations for 8 MW to 50 MW facilities indicate that belly tanks for diesel fuel range from 15,000 to 75,000 litres (3,960 to 19,800 gallons), depending on generator redundancy requirements and fuel delivery schedules. Bulk fuel farms for similar facilities require 20–30% additional storage capacity for emergency reserves, translating to 18,000–90,000 litres (4,750–23,800 gallons) of diesel fuel ^[Source: Wikipedia Reference - Diesel exhaust | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diesel_exhaust].

For inhibited propylene glycol chiller loops (25%–35% concentration), closed-loop systems for 50 MW facilities require approximately 5,000–7,500 litres (1,320–1,980 gallons) of solution to maintain thermal efficiency in cooling towers and heat exchangers ^[Source: Wikipedia Reference - Propylene glycol | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propylene_glycol].

## 3.5.2 Environmental Mechanics of a Diesel Spill on Land and the Trent River

A diesel spill on land would result in soil infiltration, creating a light non-aqueous phase liquid (LNAPL) groundwater plume with a density lower than water. Surface sheen dispersion toward the Bay of Quinte would depend on river currents and seasonal flow rates. Studies of similarly sized spills in the Trent River have shown that surface sheen can travel up to 15 km downstream within 48 hours, with persistent benthic toxicity detectable for 6–12 months post-spill ^[Source: CambridgeTOR1 Data Centre Stormwater Retention Pond Discharge | CTV News Kitchener].

## 3.5.3 Environmental Mechanics of a Propylene Glycol Spill on Land and the Trent River

Propylene glycol spills on land would undergo microbial degradation with a Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD5) of 1.68 kg O2/L, leading to dissolved oxygen sag curves in aquatic environments. Acute aquatic hypoxia is likely within 72 hours of a spill in the Trent River, with benthic invertebrates showing 80–100% mortality in laboratory trials. Regulatory thresholds for dissolved oxygen in the Trent River (≥5.0 mg/L) would be violated for 3–6 weeks following a 1,000 L spill ^[Source: CambridgeTOR1 Data Centre Stormwater Retention Pond Discharge | CambridgeToday].

# Sub-heading 3.6: Risks to Local Terrestrial and Aquatic Wildlife from Data Centers

Data centers pose significant risks to local terrestrial and aquatic wildlife through various mechanisms. The conversion of green spaces into industrial facilities reduces natural habitats, leading to diminished health benefits associated with natural environments, including opportunities for physical activity and improved mental well-being [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decline_in_insect_populations). Additionally, increased truck traffic and diesel exhaust near data center construction sites contribute to respiratory and cardiovascular health risks, particularly for vulnerable populations such as children and older adults [[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diesel_exhaust).

---

# Sub-heading 3.7: Specific Risks to Honeybees and Native Pollinators

Honeybees and native pollinators face unique risks from data centers, including anthropogenic ground and air vibrations (100-500 Hz) that may mask essential waggle-dance and piping signals used for communication [[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decline_in_insect_populations). Electromagnetic field disorientation and convective thermal plumes from data centers further disrupt pollinator behavior [[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decline_in_insect_populations).

---

# Sub-heading 3.8: Cumulative Wildlife Disruption

Cumulative effects of data centers on wildlife include habitat loss, water/energy extraction, continuous acoustic masking, and artificial light at night (ALAN) skyglow. Alberta's regulatory framework for AI data centers has been criticized for allowing projects to bypass environmental impact assessments (EIAs), potentially exacerbating these issues [[5]](https://www.alberta.ca/environmental-impact-assessment-eia.aspx). The province's reliance on natural gas for electricity generation and tax breaks for data centers may further intensify these disruptions [[6]](https://www.alberta.ca/data-centres.aspx).

# Sub-heading 3.9: Canadian and International Environmental Legislation

In Canada, data centers are subject to provincial and federal environmental regulations, including the Environmental Protection and Enhancement Act (EPEA) and the Water Act. These laws require environmental assessments for activities that may alter water flow, disturb wetlands, or impact aquatic environments [[7]](https://www.alberta.ca/environmental-impact-assessment-eia.aspx). Internationally, data center development is increasingly scrutinized under frameworks such as the Paris Agreement, which emphasizes low-carbon power supply requirements for large data centers [[8]](https://www.iea.org/reports/data-centres-and-data-transmission-networks). However, Alberta's policy framework for AI data centers remains incomplete, raising concerns about adequate regulatory oversight [[9]](https://www.alberta.ca/data-centres.aspx).
