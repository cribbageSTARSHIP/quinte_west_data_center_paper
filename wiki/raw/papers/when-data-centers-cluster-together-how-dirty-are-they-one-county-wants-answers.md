Across the United States, thousands of data centers are seeking
to break ground — sometimes forming clusters when several are
planned near one another. How much pollution might big groups of
data centers like these create?
Typically, local zoning boards or planning commissions consider
each data-center proposal individually, not as a group. But in one
corner of Pennsylvania — where more than a dozen data centers
and power plants are being proposed — officials are trying to
change that by studying the total air-quality and health effects of all
the proposed projects together, before approving them.
“What happens if you put them all in the same valley, around the
same neighborhoods?” said Bill Gaughan, a county commissioner
in Lackawanna County, where the new study is being undertaken.
As opposition grows nationwide to the pollution caused by data
centers and the immense power plants they require, officials in
Lackawanna County said they hoped their approach might set a
new precedent for how proposals are reviewed in other
communities.
Mr. Gaughan said there are more data center proposals in his
county than anywhere else in the state, and perhaps the country.
But seldom do local officials have reliable data about how
proposals overlap, he said, or the cumulative health effects if
several data centers spring up near one another.

Lackawanna County, which is home to the city of Scranton,
recently approved an independent study of the air pollution and
health effects that are likely to result from the cluster of data
centers and fossil-fuel-burning power plants proposed there. The
study, to be completed by EmPower Analytics Group by the end of
the year, will assess how much pollution the facilities would add,
where it would go, who would be exposed to it and what that could
mean for public health countywide.
“People don’t breathe one permit at a time,” said Michael Cork, a
Harvard-trained environmental health scientist who founded
EmPower, and Lackawanna County amounts to “a major proposed
infrastructure build-out, concentrated in one county.”
The study will also identify choices that developers could make to
reduce public health harms, like the types of generator engines,
limits on how long they run and considerations for where they
would be placed.
In a separate study released in July, EmPower found that a single
data center proposal in Lackawanna County, called Wildcat Ridge,
that proposed a collection of 588 diesel backup generators could
“produce meaningful adverse public health impacts” and $14
million in annual health-related damages based on what it called
relatively limited operation. If dirtier generators were to be used
more often, the research found, those public health damages
could rise as high as $124 million, with as many as eight additional
premature deaths per year.
