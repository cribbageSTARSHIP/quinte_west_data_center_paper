How do data centres affect water sources?
Computing equipment generates a lot of heat, so facilities use
water to cool it. Many cooling systems then evaporate that water to
expel the heat, meaning it may not return to the water cycle in the
same place. 
"They can pull water from a watershed, that water can evaporate
off, become a cloud, that cloud will float away and will become rain
in a totally different region where that water doesn't get returned to
the [original region's] watershed," Pasek said. 
Smaller-scale operations can draw tens of thousands of litres a
day, while hyperscale operations can draw more than a million.
A 2025 report by the U.K. Government Digital Sustainability
Alliance found that a single 100-megawatt hyperscale facility could
consume about 2.5 billion litres of water annually — enough to
serve 80,000 people.

Are there health risks living near data centres? 
Experts say there can be, but the risks vary depending on the data
centre's size, design and power source, meaning that different
data centres can carry vastly different risks.  
Air pollution
Data centres can draw electricity from local grids, but it can take
years to fully connect them. As a result, many are now being
designed with on-site power plants fuelled by natural gas or
methane gas. When powered by fossil fuels, data centres produce
emissions that contribute to climate change, as well as a type of
air pollution called fine particulate matter, also known as PM2.5.

Wildfire smoke is one of the main sources of PM2.5, a dangerous

"We do know that exposure to fine particulate matter increases the
risk of all kinds of [negative] health outcomes, from respiratory,
cardiovascular and neurocognitive," Francesca Dominici, a
professor of biostatistics at Harvard Chan School of Public Health,
told CBC News. She added that chronic exposure to air pollution
can also increase annual hospital visits and worsen asthma. 
An independent analysis she worked on with EmPower Analytics
Group examined a specific AI data centre steps from a residential
area in northern Virginia and found its eight natural gas turbines
and 51 diesel generators could result in $53 million to $99 million
US a year in health-related damages. 
Studies have suggested that chronic exposure to PM2.5 may also
be linked to a higher risk of lung cancer. 
Noise pollution
Like other industrial buildings, data centres produce noise around
the clock from fans and backup diesel generators. They can also
emit a low-frequency hum, and chronic exposure can cause
stress, sleep problems and long-term health complications,
Dominici said.
According to NBC News, residents have filed at least 15 lawsuits
in 12 U.S. states over constant data centre noise in the past three
years.

Are there alternatives to traditional big-box
data centres?
Experts say it's a question of trade-offs. One option is to make
data centres more sustainable.  
The health risks associated with air pollution, for example, can be
mitigated if a data centre and its backup power systems use wind
and solar instead of fossil fuels. 
"If you could make the facilities optimally efficient and power them
with renewable energy, either in the moment or stored in batteries
or other types of storage, we'd be making some real progress
there," Daniel Bresette, president of the U.S.-based Environmental
and Energy Study Institute, told CBC News. 
But that approach is more expensive, meaning developers may
have little incentive to adopt it without regulations or community
pressure. Most new projects in Alberta are set to use natural gas,
including a $13-billion AI data centre project announced by Meta
last month.
