Data centers use up to millions of gallons of water a day,

In light of these concerns, Shinsuke Tanaka, assistant professor of
agricultural and resource economics in the College of Agriculture,
Health and Natural Resources (CAHNR), an expert on health and
environmental economics, weighs in on how these issues are
impacting human and environmental health.
Connecticut began offering tax incentives for developers to build
data centers in the state starting in 2021. Since then, local
opposition to data center projects has grown. Groton, West Haven,
and Morris have instituted moratoriums on data center
construction in their communities. Many others are weighing
implementing their own bans.
“This is really not just a local issue,” Tanaka says.
One of the largest issues when it comes to data centers and
health, Tanaka says, is that data centers are contributing to
increased air pollution.
“The biggest issue is that data centers use a lot of energy,” Tanaka
says. “Currently, fossil fuels are still used as a major energy
source, and they emit pollution as electricity is generated.”
Tanaka has previously demonstrated that downwind pollution from
power plants led to higher COVID-19 mortality rates. According to
the World Health Organization, air pollution has also been linked to
a host of other health conditions.
Because energy is generated regionally, the impact of data centers
extends beyond the area immediately surrounding a center.

“Air pollution has a huge impact on human health,” Tanaka says.
“There are a lot of studies on this topic. The data centers are just
increasing and expanding and the trajectory is skyrocketing, and
the concern about air pollution is not just local, but it’s regional.”
On a local level, data centers are equipped with diesel generators
for backup power, creating air pollution there as well.
Because the data center boom is so new, there has not yet been a
comprehensive study analyzing how much more electricity and
hence pollution is being generated to power them. But a Cornell
University study projects data centers will put an additional 24 to
44 million metric tons of carbon dioxide into the atmosphere by
2030.
While the exact numbers may be unclear, researchers already
know a lot about the health impacts of air pollution.
“The concerns that come from increased energy consumption and
increased pollution in terms of how that affects local health and the
environment – it is known,” Tanaka says. “We can extrapolate
evidence from different contexts to data centers.”
Another consideration is that data centers are built where land is
cheap. This means that these areas are generally already under-
resourced communities, and often communities of color. This can
exacerbate existing health disparities.
“They’re the ones that are affected by the existence of data
centers,” Tanaka says.
Data centers also produce 24/7 noise pollution which, based on
studies looking at noise pollution from wind turbines and airports,
has been linked to increased rates of depression and suicide.

Noise pollution also leads to lower home values.
Further, data centers’ water usage has become an even more
serious concern as many regions face hotter, drier climates. This
could also lead to higher water utility prices for consumers.
“They’re consuming a lot of water,” Tanaka says. “Especially now,
we’ve seen a lot of hot air and a lack of rain, and this year a lot of
regions faced drought. So, that has been another concern.”
There are ways that data centers can be run more sustainably,
Tanaka explains. For example, there is a push to require data
centers to use battery storage for backup power instead of diesel
generators, which can use energy more efficiently and do not
directly produce air pollution. Data centers can also draw more of
their electricity from renewable forms of energy.
“I think we can achieve both economic prosperity along with
environmental sustainability and protection,” says Tanaka. “The
key isn’t just prohibiting data centers. So, we need to think about
how to generate electricity.”
This work relates to CAHNR’s Strategic Vision area focused
on Enhancing Health and Well-Being Locally, Nationally, and
