## Potential Power Draw from a Data Center at 7 Riverside Drive, Trenton: A Comprehensive Analysis  


![Figure 1.6: Site Infrastructure and Feeder Approach Map — 7 Riverside Drive](wiki/raw/maps/quinte_west_Saputo_data_center.svg){width=90%}

### Introduction  
The rapid proliferation of data centers in Canada, particularly in regions like the Trent River / Bay of Quinte watershed, has raised critical questions about infrastructure capacity, environmental impact, and regulatory alignment. This paper examines the potential power draw from a proposed data center at **7 Riverside Drive, Trenton**, a site formerly occupied by the Saputo Dairy facility, and its implications for the local power grid, municipal infrastructure, and ecological systems. Drawing on technical data from utility networks, environmental impact assessments, and regulatory frameworks, this analysis evaluates the feasibility of hosting a large-scale data center in this region, with specific attention to the **Trenton Frankford Road site** (former Sonoco Paper Mill) and the **Riverside Drive site**.  

---

### Section 1: Power Infrastructure Context in the Trenton Area  

#### 1.1 Existing Electrical Grid Capacity  
The electrical infrastructure serving Trenton and surrounding areas in Hastings County is part of a broader regional grid managed by local utilities and provincial energy regulators. Key metrics from the **Hydro One** network (Source [1]) reveal critical parameters for assessing power availability:  

- **Thorold TS (Transformer Station)**:  
  - **Voltage**: 13.8 kV  
  - **Minimum Load**: 12.0 MW  
  - **Thermal Capacity**: 6.1 MW (per bus)  
  - **Short Circuit Capacity**: 60.0 MVA  

- **Tilbury West DS (Distribution Substation)**:  
  - **Voltage**: 27.6 kV  
  - **Minimum Load**: 5.8 MW  
  - **Thermal Capacity**: 14.3 MW  
  - **Short Circuit Capacity**: 429.5 MVA  

These figures indicate that the existing grid infrastructure in the Trenton area has **significant capacity** for high-load facilities, though localized constraints may exist depending on proximity to substations and feeder lines. For instance, the **Thorold TS** has a **total thermal capacity of 6.1 MW** across its buses, which would need to be augmented for a large-scale data center.  

#### 1.2 Feeder Line Constraints  
Feeder lines connecting substations to end-users are critical bottlenecks. For example, the **Thorold TS BY M5, M6** feeder line has a **thermal capacity of 5.9 MW** (Source [1]). A data center requiring **100+ MW** (as typical for hyperscale facilities; Source [11]) would necessitate **multiple feeder connections** or upgrades to existing infrastructure. This raises questions about the **technical feasibility** of hosting a hyperscale facility at 7 Riverside Drive without significant grid modernization.  

#### 1.3 Proximity to Key Infrastructure  
The **7 Riverside Drive site** is located near the **Trent-Severn Waterway**, a historically significant area (Source [7]), and adjacent to the **Trent River / Bay of Quinte watershed**. This proximity could influence both **cooling system design** (reliance on water for heat dissipation; Source [6]) and **environmental permitting** requirements.  

---

### Section 2: Power Demand Projections for Data Centers  

#### 2.1 Traditional vs. AI-Driven Data Centers  
Modern data centers, particularly those supporting **AI workloads**, consume significantly more power than traditional facilities. As noted by **Anne Pasek** (Canada Research Chair in Media, Culture, and the Environment at Trent University), **AI data centers often require hundreds of megawatts**, compared to **2–5 MW** for conventional facilities (Source [3]). For example:  
- **Hyperscale data centers** (≥50 MW) are now standard for AI operations, with some facilities consuming **100+ MW** (equivalent to powering 75,000 homes; Source [11]).  
- **Cooling demands** are exacerbated by **GPUs** (graphic processing units), which generate **excessive heat** and require **intensive cooling systems** (Source [6]).  

#### 2.2 Power Requirements for the Trenton Site  
If a hyperscale data center were to be constructed at **7 Riverside Drive**, its power demand would need to be evaluated against:  
- **Grid capacity**: The **Thorold TS** and **Tilbury West DS** have **combined thermal capacities** of **~20 MW**, far below the **100+ MW** required for a hyperscale facility (Source [1], [11]).  
- **Feeder line upgrades**: Connecting to the grid would require **new substations** or **reinforced feeders**, which could be costly and time-consuming (Source [8]).  

#### 2.3 Comparative Analysis: Canada’s Data Center Landscape  
Canada hosts **five hyperscale data centers** as of 2026, with **two in British Columbia** (Source [11]). These facilities are typically located near **hydroelectric resources** (e.g., BC’s 500+ MW supercluster; Source [11]). In contrast, **Ontario’s grid** relies on a mix of **nuclear, hydro, and renewable energy**, but **local capacity** may be insufficient for large-scale data centers without **interconnection to provincial grids** (Source [12]).  

---

### Section 3: Environmental and Regulatory Considerations  

#### 3.1 Water Usage and Cooling Systems  
Data centers require **vast amounts of water** for cooling, with **Arizona’s data centers** using **~1 billion gallons annually** (Source [9]). In Ontario, **water metering** is critical for compliance, as seen in **Quebec’s** past oversight (Source [9]). For the **Trenton site**:  
- **Proximity to the Trent River** could allow **direct water cooling**, but **ecological impacts** on the **Bay of Quinte watershed** must be assessed (Source [4]).  
- **Wastewater discharge** and **thermal pollution** risks would need to be mitigated through **closed-loop cooling systems** or **recycling infrastructure** (Source [6]).  

#### 3.2 Regulatory Frameworks and Economic Development  
Ontario’s **data center regulatory pillars** (Source [8]) emphasize:  
1. **Economic development**: High-value jobs and investment.  
2. **Digital sovereignty**: Securing data infrastructure.  
3. **Environmental sustainability**: Minimizing ecological footprints.  

For the **7 Riverside Drive site**, alignment with these pillars would require:  
- **Job creation** in Hastings County.  
- **Local supplier participation** (e.g., using **Ontario-made equipment**).  
- **Compliance with provincial water use caps** (Source [9]).  

#### 3.3 Community and Municipal Concerns  
The **City of Quinte West** and **Hastings County** have shown **cautious interest** in data center development, given the **backlash** against similar projects in **Oakville and Mississauga** (Source [5]). Key concerns include:  
- **Land use conflicts**: The **former Saputo Dairy site** may have **soil contamination** or **zoning restrictions**.  
- **Public health risks**: Proximity to residential areas and **air quality impacts** from cooling systems (Source [4]).  

---

### Section 4: Technical and Economic Feasibility  

#### 4.1 Grid Modernization Requirements  
Hosting a **100+ MW** data center at 7 Riverside Drive would require:  
- **New substations** or **grid upgrades** to handle **peak loads**.  
- **Dedicated transmission lines** to connect to **provincial grids** (e.g., **Elexicon Energy’s service area**; Source [10]).  

#### 4.2 Cost-Benefit Analysis  
- **Capital costs**: Upgrading the grid could exceed **$50 million** (Source [12]).  
- **Operational costs**: **Hydroelectric power** (if available) would be cheaper than **grid-purchased electricity**, but **interconnection fees** may apply (Source [11]).  

#### 4.3 Alternative Energy Solutions  
- **Solar + storage**: A **50 MW solar farm** could offset **50%** of a data center’s demand, though **land use** and **seasonal variability** are challenges.  
- **Hydroelectric interconnection**: Leveraging **Ontario’s hydro resources** (e.g., **Lake Ontario** or **St. Lawrence River**) could provide **renewable energy**, but **transmission costs** would be high (Source [12]).

### Section 6: Conclusion and Recommendations  

The **potential power draw** from a data center at **7 Riverside Drive, Trenton** would necessitate **significant upgrades** to the local grid, given the **existing infrastructure’s limitations**. While the **Trenton area’s proximity to hydro resources** and **regulatory support for economic development** (Source [8]) present opportunities, **environmental risks** (e.g., **water usage**, **ecological disruption**) and **community opposition** must be addressed.  

**Recommendations**:  
1. **Conduct a grid impact assessment** to identify **feasible upgrades** for **100+ MW** capacity.  
2. **Prioritize renewable energy integration** (e.g., **hydroelectric interconnection**) to align with **Ontario’s sustainability goals**.  
3. **Engage local stakeholders** to address **land use, health, and environmental concerns**.  
4. **Leverage provincial incentives** for **digital sovereignty** and **economic development** (Source [8]).  

By balancing **technical feasibility**, **environmental stewardship**, and **community needs**, the **Trenton area** could become a **strategic hub** for **AI-driven data centers** in Ontario.
