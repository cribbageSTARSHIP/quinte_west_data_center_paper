## Requirement 1.2: How Governments Plan Power Needs Around Data Centers  

### Introduction  

Data centers have emerged as critical infrastructure for modern economies, underpinning digital services, cloud computing, and artificial technical (AI) development. However, their energy demands pose significant challenges for power grid planning, environmental sustainability, and municipal infrastructure management. Governments must balance the need for reliable power supply with the imperative to mitigate climate impacts and ensure equitable access to energy resources. This paper examines how governments plan power needs around data centers, drawing on technical, regulatory, and environmental insights from authoritative sources. The analysis is contextualized with reference to the City of Quinte West, Hastings County, the Trent River / Bay of Quinte watershed, and proposed data center sites at 7 Riverside Drive (former Saputo Dairy) and 920 Trenton Frankford Road (former Sonoco Paper Mill).  

---

### Technical Mechanisms for Power Demand Management in Data Centers  

Data centers are energy-intensive facilities, consuming approximately 1-2% of global electricity demand (Source [1], [3]). Their operational requirements—such as cooling systems, server redundancy, and high availability—necessitate robust power planning. Governments and grid operators must address these demands through innovative mechanisms, including demand response (DR) programs, grid capacity expansions, and energy efficiency standards.  

#### 1. **Demand Response (DR) and Pricing Mechanisms**  

Emergency demand response (EDR) programs are critical for stabilizing power grids during peak loads or emergencies. Traditionally, data centers have relied on backup diesel generators for EDR, which is costly and environmentally harmful (Source [1], [3]). Recent research proposes alternative mechanisms, such as **ColoEDR**, a pricing model designed for multi-tenant (colocation) data centers. ColoEDR leverages **parameterized supply function bidding** to incentivize tenants to reduce load during emergencies, thereby fulfilling energy reduction requirements without relying on fossil fuels. This approach provides **near-optimal efficiency guarantees** for both price-taking and price-anticipating tenants (Source [1], [3]).  

For example, in Alberta, the government’s push to become a leader in AI-driven data centers (Source [5]) includes provisions for integrating DR programs into grid planning. Alberta’s Memorandum of Understanding (MOU) with the federal government emphasizes the need for **low-carbon power supply** to meet data center demands while achieving net-zero emissions by 2050 (Source [5]). This aligns with ColoEDR’s goals of reducing reliance on diesel generators and promoting sustainable energy use.  

#### 2. **Grid Capacity and Infrastructure Planning**  

Data centers often require significant grid capacity, necessitating upgrades to transmission lines, substations, and distribution networks. In Alberta, the **Alberta Electric System Operator (AESO)** has set a **1,200 MW limit** for data center allocations, reflecting the need to balance growth with grid reliability (Source [9]). Similarly, in Ontario, the **Independent Electricity System Operator (IESO)** has introduced **technical requirements** for large computational loads (e.g., data centers), including compliance with **market rules**, **transmission system codes (TSC)**, and **distribution system codes (DSC)** (Source [6]). These requirements mandate that data center applicants provide detailed project data, such as **load composition**, **voltage and frequency operating ranges**, and **project development plans** (Source [6]).  

In Quinte West and Hastings County, similar infrastructure planning would be essential for the proposed data centers at 7 Riverside Drive and 920 Trenton Frankford Road. These sites, located near the Trent River / Bay of Quinte watershed, may require assessments of **existing grid capacity**, **potential transmission upgrades**, and **environmental impacts** on local ecosystems. For instance, the **Trent River** is a vital waterway for biodiversity and recreation, and any grid expansion must avoid disrupting its ecological integrity (Source [8]).

### Regulatory Frameworks and Market Participation  

Governments regulate data center power needs through legal frameworks, market rules, and policy incentives. These frameworks aim to ensure grid reliability, promote renewable energy integration, and manage the economic implications of large-scale data center operations.  

#### 1. **Legal and Policy Instruments**  

In Ontario, the **Affordable Energy Act, 2024 (SO 2024, c 26)** introduces provisions for **cost responsibility rules** in high-growth areas, ensuring that new infrastructure investments are equitable (Source [2]). Similarly, Alberta’s **Electric Utilities Act** includes **Data Centre Regulations** that govern interconnection processes, requiring approval from the **Alberta Utilities Commission (AUC)** for new transmission lines or substation upgrades (Source [9]).  

For Quinte West, Hastings County, and the Trent River / Bay of Quinte watershed, analogous regulations would be necessary. For example, the **City of Quinte West** might need to establish **zoning laws** and **environmental impact assessments (EIAs)** for the proposed data centers, ensuring compliance with provincial and federal energy policies. The **Trent River**’s proximity to the 920 Trenton Frankford Road site (former Sonoco Paper Mill) would necessitate **wetland protection** and **stormwater management** plans to mitigate potential ecological disruptions (Source [8]).  

#### 2. **Market Participation and Pricing Models**  

Data centers can participate in electricity markets as **Price Responsive Load (PRL)**, a resource type that allows them to manage energy use based on **hourly Locational Marginal Pricing (LMP)** and **day-ahead schedules** (Source [12]). This model provides **financial certainty** and **operational flexibility** compared to traditional Non-Dispatchable Load (NDL) participation (Source [12]).  

In Alberta, the **Market Rule Changes (MRP)** have introduced PRL as a new resource type, enabling data centers to engage in **wholesale electricity markets** (Source [12]). For Quinte West, adopting similar PRL mechanisms could help data centers at 7 Riverside Drive and 920 Trenton Frankford Road optimize energy costs and reduce strain on the grid. However, this would require collaboration with provincial grid operators, such as the **Ontario Energy Board (OEB)**, to align with **market rules** and **settlement processes** (Source [6]).

### Environmental and Sustainability Considerations  

Data centers’ environmental impact is a critical concern for governments, particularly in regions with sensitive ecosystems like the Trent River / Bay of Quinte watershed.  

#### 1. **Cooling Systems and Energy Efficiency**  

Data centers consume vast amounts of energy for **cooling systems**, which account for up to 40% of their total energy use (Source [1], [7]). To mitigate this, governments and operators are promoting **energy-efficient cooling technologies**, such as **liquid immersion cooling** and **free cooling** (Source [7]). Alberta’s AI data center initiatives emphasize **sustainable cooling** as part of its net-zero emissions goal (Source [5]).  

In Quinte West, the proposed data centers could adopt similar technologies to reduce their environmental footprint. For instance, the **Trent River**’s **cold water temperatures** could be leveraged for **free cooling**, minimizing reliance on energy-intensive air conditioning systems (Source [8]).  

#### 2. **Renewable Energy Integration**  

Governments are increasingly requiring data centers to integrate **renewable energy sources** into their power supply. Alberta’s MOU with the federal government includes plans for **large transmission interties** with British Columbia and Saskatchewan to supply **low-carbon power** to data centers (Source [5]). Similarly, Ontario’s **IESO** has introduced **renewable energy procurement programs** to support grid decarbonization (Source [4]).  

For Quinte West, integrating **local renewable energy sources**—such as **solar farms** or **wind turbines**—could reduce the environmental impact of the proposed data centers. The **Bay of Quinte** region has significant **solar potential**, and the **Trent River**’s proximity to the 920 Trenton Frankford Road site could facilitate **hydroelectric microgrids** (Source [8]).

### Case Study: Quinte West and Hastings County  

The proposed data centers at **7 Riverside Drive** (former Saputo Dairy) and **920 Trenton Frankford Road** (former Sonoco Paper Mill) present unique challenges and opportunities for power planning.  

#### 1. **Grid Infrastructure Needs**  

Both sites are located in **Hastings County**, which is part of the **Trent River / Bay of Quinte watershed**. The region’s **existing grid infrastructure** may not support the high power demands of data centers without upgrades. For example, the **former Saputo Dairy site** (7 Riverside Drive) is near the **Trent River**, which could be used for **free cooling** but requires **stormwater management** to prevent contamination (Source [8]).  

#### 2. **Environmental Impact Assessments**  

The **Trent River** is a **designated ecological reserve** and a **key habitat for endangered species** (Source [8]). Any data center development must include **mitigation measures** to protect water quality and biodiversity. This could involve **wetland buffers**, **reduced runoff**, and **renewable energy offsets** (Source [8]).  

#### 3. **Policy and Market Alignment**  

Quinte West would need to align with **provincial energy policies** to ensure compliance with **grid codes** and **market rules**. For instance, the **Ontario Energy Board (OEB)** could require data centers to participate in **demand response programs** or **renewable energy procurement** (Source [6]). The **former Sonoco Paper Mill site** (920 Trenton Frankford Road) could leverage **existing industrial infrastructure** to reduce costs, but must also address **legacy contamination** from paper mill operations (Source [8]).  

---

### Conclusion  

Governments must adopt a multifaceted approach to planning power needs around data centers, integrating **technical innovations**, **regulatory frameworks**, and **environmental safeguards**. The case of **Quinte West** and **Hastings County** highlights the importance of **grid capacity assessments**, **renewable energy integration**, and **ecological protection** in the Trent River / Bay of Quinte watershed. By learning from Alberta’s **ColoEDR mechanisms** and Ontario’s **IESO requirements**, local authorities can ensure that data center development supports **economic growth** while minimizing **environmental harm**. Future research should focus on **localized energy modeling** and **community engagement** to tailor power planning strategies to the unique needs of regions like Quinte West.
