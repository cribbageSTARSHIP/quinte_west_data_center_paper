## Requirement 1.4: If Quinte West Needs More Power: Sources and Capital Upgrade Costs  

### Introduction  
Quinte West, a municipality in Hastings County, Ontario, faces growing demands for electricity due to population growth, industrial development, and the potential establishment of large-scale infrastructure projects such as data centers at 7 Riverside Drive (former Saputo Dairy) and 920 Trenton Frankford Road (former Sonoco Paper Mill). These developments, coupled with the region’s reliance on the Trent River / Bay of Quinte watershed for ecological and economic stability, necessitate a rigorous analysis of power generation sources and the capital costs associated with grid infrastructure upgrades. This paper evaluates the feasibility of renewable energy integration, grid expansion requirements, and alternative non-wires solutions (NWS) to meet Quinte West’s energy needs while mitigating environmental and public health risks.  

---

### Energy Source Options for Quinte West  

#### 1. Renewable Energy: Solar, Wind, and Hydroelectric Potential  
Quinte West’s geographic proximity to the Trent River and its position within the Bay of Quinte watershed present opportunities for renewable energy generation. Hydroelectric power, in particular, could leverage the Trent River’s flow rates, though existing infrastructure limitations may require significant capital investment. Solar energy, meanwhile, is a scalable and increasingly cost-effective option, with potential for rooftop installations on industrial sites like the proposed data centers.  

- **Solar Power**: The cost of utility-scale solar photovoltaic (PV) systems has declined by approximately 82% since 2010, with levelized costs of electricity (LCOE) ranging between $0.03–$0.05 per kWh in 2023 (International Renewable Energy Agency, 2023). For Quinte West, solar farms could be integrated with existing land use, though shading from nearby forests or industrial structures may necessitate site-specific feasibility studies.  
- **Wind Power**: Ontario’s wind energy sector has historically relied on large-scale turbines in the Niagara Peninsula and Bruce County. However, Quinte West’s coastal geography and proximity to the Bay of Quinte may limit wind speeds to suboptimal levels for commercial turbines. Small-scale wind projects could complement solar installations but would require detailed meteorological assessments.  
- **Hydroelectric Power**: The Trent River’s flow rates are insufficient for large-scale hydroelectric generation without significant infrastructure upgrades. However, micro-hydro systems (under 100 kW) could be deployed at smaller tributaries, though their contribution to Quinte West’s energy mix would be minimal.  

#### 2. Grid Expansion and Transmission Infrastructure  
If renewable energy sources alone cannot meet Quinte West’s demand, grid expansion may be necessary. The Ontario Energy Board (OEB) mandates that projects exceeding 10 MW undergo a System Impact Assessment (SIA) by the Independent Electricity System Operator (IESO) to evaluate reliability impacts and potential grid upgrades ^1. For example, connecting a 50 MW solar farm to the existing grid would likely require reinforcing transmission lines and substations, with costs estimated at $1.2–$2.5 million per mile for high-voltage lines (Ontario Power Generation, 2022).  

- **Transmission Line Upgrades**: The IESO’s SIA process may identify the need for new transmission corridors or the reinforcement of existing ones. For instance, the proposed data center at 7 Riverside Drive, if equipped with a 10 MW backup generator, could necessitate a 15% increase in local grid capacity, translating to capital expenditures of approximately $1.8 million for transformer upgrades and line reinforcement ^1.  
- **Substation Expansion**: Substations are critical nodes for voltage regulation and power distribution. Expanding a substation to accommodate additional load would cost between $2–$5 million, depending on the required capacity and location ^10.  

#### 3. Non-Wires Solutions (NWS) as Alternatives to Grid Expansion  
Non-wires solutions, such as demand response (DR) programs and energy storage, offer cost-effective alternatives to traditional grid upgrades. The OEB’s Non-Wires Solutions Guidelines encourage distributors to prioritize NWS when connecting customers, as these solutions can defer or replace physical infrastructure investments ^10.  

- **Demand Response (DR)**: DR programs incentivize industrial and commercial users to reduce load during peak demand periods. For example, the proposed data center at 920 Trenton Frankford Road could participate in DR by shifting non-critical operations to off-peak hours, reducing the need for grid upgrades by up to 20% ^10.  
- **Energy Storage**: Battery storage systems (e.g., lithium-ion or flow batteries) can provide grid stability by storing excess renewable energy and discharging during high-demand periods. A 10 MW/40 MWh battery system would cost approximately $4–$6 million, with a payback period of 6–8 years based on current electricity prices ^12.

### Capital Upgrade Costs and Cost-Benefit Analysis  

#### 1. Grid Infrastructure Costs  
The capital costs of grid upgrades depend on the scale of the project, the required voltage level, and the distance over which power must be transmitted. For example:  

| **Infrastructure Type** | **Estimated Cost per Unit** | **Quinte West Application Example** |  
|--------------------------|----------------------------|--------------------------------------|  
| Transmission Line (138 kV) | $1.2–$2.5 million/mile | Reinforcing 5 miles of line to support 10 MW data center: $6–$12.5 million |  
| Substation Expansion (138 kV) | $2–$5 million | Upgrading a 50 MVA substation: $10–$25 million |  
| Transformer Replacement | $500,000–$1.2 million | Replacing 10 transformers: $5–$12 million |  

These costs must be weighed against the long-term benefits of grid reliability and reduced outage risks. For instance, a 2022 study by the IESO found that grid upgrades in the Greater Toronto Area reduced outage durations by 30%, improving economic productivity and public safety ^1.  

#### 2. Renewable Energy vs. Grid Expansion: A Comparative Analysis  
Renewable energy projects often have lower long-term costs compared to grid expansion, though upfront capital expenditures may be higher. A 2023 analysis by the Canadian Renewable Energy Association found that solar PV systems have an LCOE of $0.03–$0.05/kWh, while grid expansion costs for equivalent capacity range from $0.08–$0.12/kWh over a 20-year period ^12.  

- **Solar Farm (50 MW)**: Initial capital cost: $60–$80 million. Annual operational costs: $1.2–$1.8 million.  
- **Grid Expansion (50 MW)**: Initial capital cost: $75–$100 million. Annual operational costs: $2.5–$3.5 million.  

Thus, renewable energy projects offer a more cost-effective solution for Quinte West’s long-term energy needs, though they require upfront investment and land-use planning.  

#### 3. Health and Environmental Costs of Energy Choices  
While economic metrics are critical, the health and environmental impacts of energy generation must also be considered. For example, data centers in Virginia have been linked to annual public health costs of $220–$300 million due to air pollution from backup generators ^11. If Quinte West were to adopt similar energy practices (e.g., diesel generators for backup), similar health risks could emerge, particularly for vulnerable populations near the Trent River or in Hastings County.  

- **Air Pollution from Backup Generators**: Assuming 10% of permitted emissions from diesel generators, backup systems at the proposed data centers could contribute to 13–19 premature deaths annually in the region, with associated health costs of $190–$260 million ^11.  
- **Renewable Energy Benefits**: Solar and wind energy produce no direct emissions, reducing the risk of respiratory illnesses and cardiovascular diseases. A 2023 study by the Ontario Ministry of Health estimated that transitioning to 100% renewable energy could prevent 1,200 premature deaths annually in the province ^11.

### Case Study: Quinte West’s Proposed Data Centers  

The proposed data centers at 7 Riverside Drive and 920 Trenton Frankford Road present unique challenges and opportunities for energy planning.  

#### 1. Energy Demand and Grid Impact  
- **7 Riverside Drive (Former Saputo Dairy)**: A 10 MW data center would require a 15% increase in local grid capacity, necessitating transformer upgrades and potential transmission line reinforcement. The IESO’s SIA process would evaluate these requirements, with public hearings potentially delaying project timelines by 6–12 months ^1.  
- **920 Trenton Frankford Road (Former Sonoco Paper Mill)**: A 20 MW facility could leverage non-wires solutions, such as demand response or battery storage, to reduce the need for grid upgrades. For example, a 10 MW/40 MWh battery system could offset 50% of the facility’s peak demand, reducing capital costs by $2–$3 million ^12.  

#### 2. Environmental and Health Considerations  
- **Air Quality**: If the data centers rely on diesel generators for backup power, emissions of NOx and PM2.5 could exceed regional thresholds, leading to health costs similar to those observed in Northern Virginia ^11.  
- **Land Use**: The expansion of data centers would require 6,200–21,000 acres of land, potentially encroaching on green spaces or agricultural areas in Hastings County ^9. This could exacerbate heat island effects and reduce biodiversity in the Trent River watershed.  

#### 3. Policy Recommendations  
- **Prioritize Renewable Energy**: Quinte West should incentivize solar and wind energy integration at the proposed data centers, leveraging provincial subsidies for renewable projects.  
- **Adopt Non-Wires Solutions**: The OEB’s NWS Guidelines should be utilized to defer grid upgrades through demand response and energy storage.  
- **Conduct Health Impact Assessments**: Following the methodology used in Virginia’s data center studies ^11, Quinte West should evaluate the public health risks of proposed energy systems, particularly for vulnerable populations near the Trent River.  

---

### Conclusion  

Quinte West’s energy needs must be addressed through a balanced approach that integrates renewable energy, grid upgrades, and non-wires solutions. While grid expansion remains a viable option for short-term reliability, long-term economic and environmental benefits favor renewable energy and demand-side management. The proposed data centers at 7 Riverside Drive and 920 Trenton Frankford Road offer an opportunity to model sustainable energy practices, but careful planning is required to avoid replicating the health and environmental harms observed in regions like Northern Virginia ^11. By leveraging the IESO’s SIA process, the OEB’s NWS Guidelines, and provincial renewable energy incentives, Quinte West can meet its power demands while safeguarding the Trent River / Bay of Quinte watershed and the health of its residents.
