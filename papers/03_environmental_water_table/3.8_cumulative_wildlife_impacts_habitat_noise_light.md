# How Data Centers Affect Wildlife via Habitat Loss, Water/Energy Demand, Noise, and Light: A Comprehensive Analysis  

## Introduction  
Data centers, the backbone of the digital economy, are increasingly scrutinized for their environmental and ecological impacts. As global demand for cloud computing, artificial technical (AI), and machine learning (ML) surges, data centers consume vast amounts of energy and water, emit greenhouse gases, and generate noise and light pollution. These factors collectively threaten wildlife through habitat fragmentation, resource depletion, and disruption of natural ecosystems. This paper examines the four primary pathways through which data centers affect wildlife: **habitat loss**, **water and energy demand**, **noise pollution**, and **light pollution**. The analysis draws on authoritative evidence from peer-reviewed studies, industry reports, and technical documentation, with explicit connections to the **City of Quinte West**, **Hastings County**, the **Trent River / Bay of Quinte watershed**, and proposed data center sites at **7 Riverside Drive** (former Saputo Dairy) and **920 Trenton Frankford Road** (former Sonoco Paper Mill).  

---

## 1. Habitat Loss: Land Use and Ecosystem Fragmentation  

### 1.1 Land Consumption and Infrastructure Development  
Data centers require extensive land for construction, cooling infrastructure, and energy generation. This land use often leads to **habitat fragmentation**, **deforestation**, and **displacement of native species**. For example, the **Loudoun County, VA** data center hub, which consumed **1 billion gallons of water annually in 2023** (Robinson, 2024), is located in a region where rapid urbanization has already reduced natural habitats. While Quinte West and Hastings County are not currently host to large-scale data centers, the proposed sites at 7 Riverside Drive and 920 Trenton Frankford Road could follow similar patterns if not carefully managed.  

- **Land use for data centers** often involves clearing vegetation, which disrupts local wildlife corridors and reduces biodiversity.  
- **Infrastructure development** (e.g., roads, power lines) further fragments habitats, isolating populations and increasing vulnerability to predation or inbreeding.  

### 1.2 Impact on the Trent River / Bay of Quinte Watershed  
The **Trent River** and **Bay of Quinte** are ecologically sensitive areas, home to diverse aquatic and terrestrial species. If data centers were to be constructed near these water bodies, the associated **land clearing** and **infrastructure expansion** could degrade riparian zones, reduce water quality, and disrupt migratory pathways for fish and birds. For instance, **aquifer depletion** from groundwater extraction (a common practice for data center cooling) could lower water tables, affecting wetland ecosystems and species dependent on stable hydrology (Yañez-Barnuevo, 2025).

## 2. Water and Energy Demand: Resource Depletion and Ecological Stress  

### 2.1 Water Consumption for Cooling  
Data centers rely heavily on **water for cooling**, with **evaporative cooling systems** accounting for the majority of water use. According to a 2023 report, **Google’s self-operated data centers withdrew 29 billion liters of water annually**, of which **23 billion liters were evaporated**—nearly 80% of which was **potable water** (Talukder et al., 2026). This level of consumption is comparable to the **449 million gallons of water per day** used by all U.S. data centers in 2021, though localized impacts can be disproportionately severe (Mytton, 2021).  

- **In Quinte West and Hastings County**, where the Trent River watershed is a critical water source, large-scale data centers could exacerbate **water stress** during droughts, potentially leading to **domestic water-use restrictions** for residents (Yañez-Barnuevo, 2025).  
- **Aquifer depletion** from groundwater extraction could harm **aquatic ecosystems** in the Trent River, which supports species such as **lake sturgeon** and **northern pike**.  

### 2.2 Energy Demand and Grid-Linked Water Use  
Data centers consume **approximately 2% of global electricity** (International Energy Agency, 2023), with **indirect water use** (via electricity generation) accounting for a significant portion of their total water footprint. For example, **Microsoft reported a 34% increase in water use between 2021 and 2022**, driven by rising energy demand (Talukder et al., 2026).  

- **Power generation for data centers** often relies on **thermal power plants**, which consume water for cooling. In regions with **high water stress** (e.g., the **Southwest U.S.**), this could shift ecological burdens to **off-site water sources**, compounding local impacts (Alston et al., 2026).  
- **Stress-adjusted water metrics** (which account for both water volume and regional scarcity) reveal that **75% of U.S. data centers** experience **elevated water stress** annually, with off-site impacts dominating in major markets (Talukder et al., 2026).  

### 2.3 Mitigation Strategies for Quinte West  
To minimize water and energy impacts in Quinte West:  
- **Rainwater harvesting** could offset **100% of on-site cooling water demand** in suitable regions (Talukder et al., 2026).  
- **Dry cooling technologies**, though energy-intensive, eliminate **direct water withdrawal** (Talukder et al., 2026). However, they shift water use to **power plants**, which may be located in **water-stressed regions** (Alston et al., 2026).

## 3. Noise Pollution: Disruption of Wildlife Communication and Behavior  

### 3.1 Sources of Noise from Data Centers  
Data centers generate noise from:  
- **HVAC systems** (heating, ventilation, and air conditioning)  
- **Cooling towers** and **pumps**  
- **Generators** and **transformers**  
- **Server racks** and **data processing units**  

Noise levels can exceed **50 decibels (dB)**, comparable to **city traffic** or **conversational speech**. While noise pollution is less studied in the context of data centers, **industrial facilities** (e.g., manufacturing plants) have been shown to disrupt **animal communication**, **foraging behavior**, and **reproductive success** (National Park Service, 2022).  

### 3.2 Impact on the Trent River / Bay of Quinte Ecosystem  
- **Aquatic species** (e.g., **fish**, **amphibians**) rely on **acoustic signals** for **mating**, **predator avoidance**, and **navigation**. Chronic noise exposure could impair these behaviors.  
- **Birds** and **mammals** near data center sites (e.g., 7 Riverside Drive) may experience **stress**, **altered migration routes**, or **reduced nesting success**.  

### 3.3 Mitigation Strategies  
- **Sound barriers** and **noise-dampening materials** can reduce emissions from HVAC systems.  
- **Zoning regulations** could limit data center construction near sensitive habitats or water bodies.  

---

## 4. Light Pollution: Disruption of Circadian Rhythms and Ecosystems  

### 4.1 Sources of Light Pollution  
Data centers emit artificial light from:  
- **Building exteriors** (e.g., security lighting, signage)  
- **Cooling infrastructure** (e.g., illuminated equipment)  
- **Surveillance systems**  

Light pollution is a growing concern, with **over 80% of the world’s population** living under **light-polluted skies** (Falchi et al., 2016).  

### 4.2 Impact on Wildlife in Quinte West  
- **Nocturnal species** (e.g., **owls**, **bats**, **fireflies**) are particularly vulnerable to **disrupted circadian rhythms**, **reduced foraging efficiency**, and **increased predation risk**.  
- **Aquatic ecosystems** in the **Trent River** may be affected by **light reflection** from data center infrastructure, altering **phytoplankton growth** and **fish behavior**.  

### 4.3 Mitigation Strategies  
- **Shielded lighting** and **motion sensors** can minimize light spillage.  
- **Dark-sky policies** could restrict data center lighting near natural habitats.

## 6. Case Study: Quinte West and Hastings County  

### 6.1 Proposed Sites and Environmental Risks  
- **7 Riverside Drive** (former Saputo Dairy): Located near the **Trent River**, this site could face **water stress** if cooling systems draw from local aquifers or the Trent River itself.  
- **920 Trenton Frankford Road** (former Sonoco Paper Mill): Situated in a **rural area**, this site may disrupt **terrestrial habitats** and **migratory bird routes** if not buffered by green spaces.  

### 6.2 Recommendations for Sustainable Development  
- **Conduct ecological assessments** to identify sensitive habitats and water sources.  
- **Adopt stress-adjusted water metrics** to prioritize **rainwater harvesting** and **dry cooling** in Quinte West (Talukder et al., 2026).  
- **Implement noise and light mitigation** to protect **nocturnal wildlife** and **aquatic ecosystems**.  

---

## 7. Conclusion  

Data centers exert profound ecological impacts through **habitat loss**, **resource depletion**, **noise**, and **light pollution**. While the **Trent River / Bay of Quinte watershed** and **Quinte West** are not currently host to large-scale data centers, the proposed sites at 7 Riverside Drive and 920 Trenton Frankford Road necessitate rigorous environmental planning. By adopting **stress-adjusted water management**, **renewable energy integration**, and **ecological zoning**, stakeholders can mitigate these impacts and align data center development with **sustainability goals**.
