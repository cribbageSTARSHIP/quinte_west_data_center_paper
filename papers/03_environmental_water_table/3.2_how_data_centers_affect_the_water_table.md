# How Data Centers Affect the Water Table: A Comprehensive Analysis of Open and Closed Loop Systems  

## Introduction  
Data centers, the backbone of the digital economy, consume vast quantities of water for cooling and operational purposes. As global demand for cloud computing, artificial technical, and big data analytics surges, the environmental impact of data centers—particularly on groundwater and surface water systems—has become a critical concern for municipal planners, environmental scientists, and policymakers. This paper examines the dual mechanisms by which data centers influence the water table: **open-loop cooling systems**, which draw water from aquifers or surface sources and discharge it after use, and **closed-loop systems**, which recirculate water but may still alter hydrological dynamics through thermal pollution or chemical discharge. The analysis draws on peer-reviewed studies, industry reports, and technical assessments to quantify water usage, evaluate ecological risks, and propose mitigation strategies. Special attention is given to the **City of Quinte West, Hastings County, and the Trent River / Bay of Quinte watershed**, where proposed data center developments at **7 Riverside Drive (former Saputo Dairy)** and **920 Trenton Frankford Road (former Sonoco Paper Mill)** raise urgent questions about water sustainability.  

---

## Open-Loop Cooling Systems: Direct Water Consumption and Aquifer Depletion  

### Mechanism and Water Usage  
Open-loop cooling systems, the most common in data centers, extract water from local aquifers or surface water bodies (e.g., rivers, lakes) to cool servers and infrastructure. The water is then discharged at a higher temperature, often exceeding regulatory thresholds for thermal pollution. This process places significant strain on groundwater reserves and surface water ecosystems.  

- **Water Consumption Metrics**:  
  - A medium-sized data center (15 megawatts) consumes as much water as **three hospitals or two 18-hole golf courses** annually (Mytton, 2021; Wren, 2024; Powell, 2024)^.  
  - In **Loudoun County, Virginia**, the world’s largest data center hub consumed **1 billion gallons (3.78 billion liters)** of potable water in 2023, averaging **2.75 million gallons per day** (Robinson, 2024)^.  
  - Nationally, U.S. data centers consumed **449 million gallons per day (1.7 billion liters)** in 2021, though localized impacts in regions like Virginia and Northern Virginia (which saw a **250% increase in water use over four years**) are disproportionately severe (Mytton, 2021; S&P Global, 2025)^.  

### Impact on Groundwater and Surface Water  
Open-loop systems can lead to **aquifer depletion**, particularly in regions with limited water availability. For example:  
- **Aquifer Depletion**: Many data centers in Virginia and Texas draw water directly from municipal groundwater wells, reducing water availability for domestic and agricultural use during droughts (Yañez-Barnuevo, 2025)^.  
- **Thermal Pollution**: Discharged water, often 10–20°C warmer than ambient temperatures, can disrupt aquatic ecosystems by altering oxygen levels and harming fish populations (Currie et al., 2013; Stanke et al., 2013)^.  

### Case Study: Northern Virginia’s Water Stress  
Northern Virginia, home to over 100 data centers, faces acute water stress due to competing demands from thermoelectric power generation, agriculture, and public supply. In 2023, data centers consumed **2 billion gallons (7.57 billion liters)** of water—a **63% increase since 2019**—exacerbating competition with existing users and intensifying groundwater depletion (Sierra Club, n.d.; JLARC, 2024)^.  

### Relevance to Quinte West and the Trent River Watershed  
The **Trent River / Bay of Quinte watershed** is a critical ecological and municipal water source for Hastings County. If data centers were to adopt open-loop systems at the proposed sites (7 Riverside Drive or 920 Trenton Frankford Road), they could:  
- **Deplete local aquifers**: Groundwater extraction for cooling could reduce water availability for agriculture, municipal use, and the Trent River’s flow, which supports over 100 species of fish and invertebrates (Hastings County Environmental Assessment, 2023)^.  
- **Increase thermal pollution**: Discharged water from open-loop systems could raise the Trent River’s temperature, threatening cold-water fish species like lake sturgeon and walleye (Ontario Ministry of Natural Resources, 2022)^.

## Closed-Loop Cooling Systems: Recirculation and Indirect Hydrological Impacts  

### Mechanism and Water Efficiency  
Closed-loop systems recirculate water through cooling towers or direct-to-air systems, minimizing direct water withdrawal. However, they still require water for **evaporative cooling** and may discharge **wastewater** containing **chemicals** (e.g., antifreeze, corrosion inhibitors) or **thermal effluent**.  

- **Water Usage Metrics**:  
  - Closed-loop systems reduce water consumption by **50–90%** compared to open-loop systems, depending on efficiency (The Green Grid, 2021)^.  
  - However, **Scope 2 water use** (indirect consumption from electricity generation) remains significant, as power plants (especially thermal and nuclear) consume water for evaporative cooling (Talukder et al., 2026)^.  

### Impact on Water Quality and Hydrology  
While closed-loop systems reduce direct water withdrawal, they introduce **indirect risks**:  
- **Chemical Discharge**: Wastewater from closed-loop systems may contain **propylene glycol**, **glycols**, or **heavy metals**, which can contaminate groundwater or surface water if not treated (EPA, 1976; Sills and Blakeslee, 1992)^.  
- **Thermal Pollution**: Even in closed-loop systems, **evaporative cooling** can raise water temperatures in recirculating systems, though to a lesser extent than open-loop systems (Mytton, 2021)^.  

### Case Study: Microsoft’s Data Center in Virginia  
Microsoft’s data center, which used **700,000 liters of water to train GPT-3**, highlights the indirect water footprint of AI operations. While closed-loop systems reduce direct consumption, the **Scope 3 water footprint** (embodied water in manufacturing IT equipment) remains a challenge (Verma and Tan, 2024)^.  

### Relevance to Quinte West and the Trent River Watershed  
If closed-loop systems were implemented at the proposed Quinte West sites, the following risks could arise:  
- **Chemical Contamination**: Untreated wastewater from cooling towers could leach into the Trent River or groundwater, affecting water quality for municipal and agricultural use (EPA, 1976)^.  
- **Thermal Stress**: Even with closed-loop systems, **evaporative cooling** could raise local water temperatures, impacting aquatic life (Ontario Ministry of Natural Resources, 2022)^.

## Comparative Analysis: Open-Loop vs. Closed-Loop Systems  

| **Factor**               | **Open-Loop Systems**                          | **Closed-Loop Systems**                          |  
|--------------------------|-----------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------|  
| **Water Withdrawal**     | High (direct extraction from aquifers/surface water) | Low (recirculation minimizes withdrawal)          |  
| **Water Discharge**      | High volume of heated wastewater                | Lower volume, but may contain chemicals         |  
| **Aquifer Depletion**    | Significant risk                                | Minimal risk (if water is recirculated)         |  
| **Thermal Pollution**    | High (discharged water raises ambient temps)  | Moderate (evaporative cooling still affects temps)|  
| **Chemical Pollution**   | Low (minimal discharge)                       | Moderate (wastewater may contain chemicals)       |  
| **Mitigation Strategies**| Water recycling, alternative cooling methods | Advanced filtration, closed-loop chemical management|  

*Source: Mytton (2021), Talukder et al. (2026), EPA (1976)^.*

## Environmental and Health Implications  

### Water Scarcity and Ecosystem Stress  
Data centers contribute to **global water footprint** projections of **4.2–6.6 billion cubic meters by 2027**, exceeding the annual consumption of countries like Denmark (Li et al., 2025)^. In drought-prone regions, this demand can exacerbate **water scarcity**, leading to:  
- **Domestic water-use restrictions** during droughts (Robinson, 2024)^.  
- **Ecosystem degradation** in rivers and aquifers (Currie et al., 2013)^.  

### Health Risks  
Indirect health impacts include:  
- **Waterborne diseases**: Contaminated water from chemical discharge or thermal pollution can increase risks of diarrheal diseases (Stanke et al., 2013)^.  
- **Maternal and infant health**: Water scarcity and pollution may worsen outcomes in vulnerable populations (Currie et al., 2013)^.  

### Relevance to Quinte West  
The Trent River watershed is already under pressure from agricultural runoff and urban development. Introducing data centers could compound these stressors, risking:  
- **Reduced water quality** in the Trent River, affecting drinking water for Quinte West residents.  
- **Economic impacts** on agriculture, which relies on consistent water availability from the Trent River.

## Policy and Mitigation Strategies  

### Regulatory Frameworks  
- **Water Usage Effectiveness (WUE)**: A metric proposed by The Green Grid to quantify water efficiency (WUE = water used / IT power consumed) (Talukder et al., 2026)^.  
- **Mandatory Reporting**: Public disclosure of data center water use and pollution metrics, as recommended by the **Joint Legislative Audit and Review Commission (JLARC, 2024)**.  

### Sustainable Practices for Quinte West  
To mitigate risks at the proposed sites:  
1. **Adopt closed-loop systems** with advanced filtration to prevent chemical discharge.  
2. **Implement water recycling** to reduce reliance on groundwater.  
3. **Use renewable energy** to minimize Scope 2 water use from fossil fuel-based power generation (Talukder et al., 2026)^.  
4. **Conduct hydrological impact assessments** for the Trent River / Bay of Quinte watershed, ensuring compliance with provincial water quality standards.  

---

## Conclusion  

Data centers exert profound pressures on water tables through both open-loop and closed-loop systems, with localized impacts often exceeding national averages. In regions like **Northern Virginia**, where water demand has surged by 250% in four years, the risks of aquifer depletion and thermal pollution are stark. For **Quinte West, Hastings County**, and the **Trent River / Bay of Quinte watershed**, the proposed data center developments at 7 Riverside Drive and 920 Trenton Frankford Road must be evaluated through a lens of **sustainability, equity, and ecological preservation**. Without stringent regulations and innovative cooling technologies, the dual threats of water scarcity and pollution could undermine the region’s environmental and economic resilience.
