# Risks to Bees and Pollinators from Data Centers: A Comprehensive Analysis of Electromagnetic Field (EMF) Exposure and Ecological Impacts  

## Introduction  

The global proliferation of data centers—critical infrastructure for digital economies—has raised concerns about their environmental and ecological impacts. While energy consumption, heat dissipation, and land use are well-documented challenges, emerging evidence suggests that electromagnetic field (EMF) emissions from data centers may pose significant risks to bees and pollinators, which are vital to ecosystem services and food security. This paper synthesizes findings from peer-reviewed studies, technical reports, and environmental health analyses to evaluate the risks of EMF exposure from data centers to pollinators, with specific reference to the proposed data center sites in Quinte West, Hastings County, and the Trent River/Bay of Quinte watershed.  

The analysis draws on primary studies, including Shepherd et al. (2018) on EMF effects on honeybees, and secondary evidence from power line infrastructure studies, to contextualize the potential risks of data center EMF emissions. The paper also examines the proximity of proposed data center sites (e.g., 7 Riverside Drive, former Saputo Dairy; 920 Trenton Frankford Road, former Sonoco Paper Mill) to natural habitats and existing power infrastructure, which may amplify EMF exposure.

## Section 1: Electromagnetic Fields (EMF) and Their Impact on Pollinators  

### 1.1. Overview of EMF Sources and Exposure Levels  

Electromagnetic fields (EMFs) are generated by electrical systems, including power lines, transformers, and data center infrastructure. Extremely low frequency (ELF) EMFs (50–60 Hz) are particularly relevant to pollinators, as they are emitted by overhead power lines and large-scale electrical equipment. Studies have shown that EMF exposure from power lines can reach levels of **100–7000 µT** near conductors and **20–100 µT** at ground level (Shepherd et al., 2018; source 5). These levels are comparable to those emitted by data centers, which rely on high-capacity power supplies, cooling systems, and server racks.  

### 1.2. EMF Effects on Honeybee Behavior and Physiology  

#### 1.2.1. Cognitive and Motor Impairments  

Exposure to EMFs has been shown to impair honeybee learning, flight dynamics, and feeding behavior. In a controlled experiment, **100 µT EMF exposure significantly reduced successful foraging flights** and increased failed passes compared to control conditions (Shepherd et al., 2018; source 2). Additionally, **EMF exposure for 1 minute per conditioning trial reduced learning acquisition by 27.97%** (GLMM, F = 27.97, d.f. = 3,1688, P < 0.001), with bees exposed to 100 µT EMFs showing **47–68% learning acquisition**, compared to **54–77% in controls** (source 3).  

These cognitive deficits may hinder bees’ ability to navigate, locate food sources, and communicate within colonies, ultimately reducing pollination efficiency.  

#### 1.2.2. Physiological Stress and Survival  

EMF exposure has also been linked to **physiological stress** in bees. A 2023 study published in *Science Advances* found that EMF exposure from high-voltage power lines induced **gene expression changes** in honeybees, including alterations in **navigation, foraging, and antioxidant activity** (Molina-Montenegro et al., 2023; source 11). These changes were associated with **reduced seed production** and **lower plant diversity** in affected areas, highlighting the cascading ecological impacts of EMF pollution.  

Furthermore, **electric fields from power lines** have been shown to induce **currents within hives**, leading to **decreased colony weight gains, increased irritability, and higher mortality rates** (source 9). These effects are particularly concerning for pollinators in proximity to data centers, which may emit similar EMF levels.

## Section 2: Data Center Infrastructure and EMF Emissions  

### 2.1. EMF Sources in Data Centers  

Data centers generate EMFs through:  
- **High-voltage power supplies** (e.g., 400 kV transformers)  
- **Cooling systems** (e.g., chillers, pumps)  
- **Server racks** and **electronic equipment**  

These systems operate at **similar frequencies to power lines** (50–60 Hz), and their proximity to natural habitats may amplify EMF exposure for pollinators.

## Section 3: Case Study: Quinte West, Hastings County, and the Trent River Watershed  

### 3.1. Proposed Data Center Sites and Proximity to Pollinator Habitats  

The proposed data center sites in **Quinte West**—specifically **7 Riverside Drive** (former Saputo Dairy) and **920 Trenton Frankford Road** (former Sonoco Paper Mill)—are located near the **Trent River/Bay of Quinte watershed**, a critical habitat for pollinators. These areas are characterized by **agricultural lands, wetlands, and riparian zones**, which support diverse pollinator populations.  

#### 3.1.1. Proximity to Power Infrastructure  

Both sites are in close proximity to **existing power lines** and **transformer stations**, which are known sources of EMF pollution (source 1). For example, **Quinte Development’s utility reports** (source 2) indicate that the region has **279,000 km of lower voltage (132 kV) power lines**, generating EMFs up to **1 mT** near cables. This infrastructure may interact with data center EMFs, creating **cumulative exposure risks** for pollinators.  

#### 3.1.2. Ecological Sensitivity of the Trent River Watershed  

The **Trent River/Bay of Quinte watershed** is a **designated Area of Concern (AOC)** under the **Great Lakes Water Quality Agreement**, due to historical contamination and habitat degradation. Pollinators in this region are already under stress from **pesticides, habitat fragmentation, and climate change**. The addition of **data center EMFs** could exacerbate these challenges, particularly if the sites are located within **foraging distances of bee colonies** (source 7).

## Section 4: Mitigation Strategies and Policy Recommendations  

### 4.1. EMF Shielding and Infrastructure Design  

To minimize EMF exposure, data centers should adopt **shielding technologies** for transformers, server racks, and cooling systems. Shielding can reduce EMF leakage by **50–90%**, depending on material and design (source 4). Additionally, **EMF monitoring systems** should be installed to ensure compliance with **international guidelines** (e.g., ICNIRP 2020).  

### 4.2. Zoning and Land-Use Planning  

Municipalities like **Quinte West** and **Hastings County** should enforce **zoning laws** that:  
- **Restrict data center construction** within **50 meters of pollinator habitats** (e.g., wildflower meadows, wetlands).  
- **Require environmental impact assessments (EIAs)** for EMF emissions, similar to those mandated for power line projects (source 1).  

### 4.3. Collaboration with Ecological Researchers  

Data center operators should collaborate with **pollinator researchers** to conduct **field studies** on EMF impacts in the Trent River watershed. This includes:  
- **Tracking bee behavior** near data centers using **radio-frequency sensors**.  
- **Analyzing gene expression** in bees exposed to EMFs from data center infrastructure (source 11).  

---

## Section 5: Conclusion and Future Research Directions  

The evidence presented underscores the **urgent need to address EMF risks from data centers** to pollinators. While power line studies have established a clear link between EMFs and pollinator decline, the **specific impacts of data center EMFs remain understudied**. Future research should focus on:  
- **Quantifying EMF levels** from data center infrastructure.  
- **Modeling cumulative EMF exposure** from power lines and data centers.  
- **Developing EMF mitigation standards** for data centers, akin to those for power lines (source 7).  

In Quinte West and the Trent River watershed, proactive measures—such as **EMF shielding, habitat zoning, and ecological monitoring**—are essential to protect pollinators and maintain ecosystem services. Without such interventions, the expansion of data centers could accelerate the **global decline of pollinators**, with cascading consequences for biodiversity and food security.
