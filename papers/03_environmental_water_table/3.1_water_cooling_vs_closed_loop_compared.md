# Water Cooling of Data Centers vs. Closed-Loop Cooling Systems Compared: A Comparative Analysis for Municipal and Environmental Planning  

## Introduction  
Data centers are pivotal to modern digital infrastructure, yet their operational demands for energy and water pose significant challenges for municipal planners, environmental scientists, and infrastructure engineers. As global demand for cloud computing, artificial technical (AI), and high-performance computing (HPC) surges, the environmental footprint of data centers—particularly their water consumption—has become a critical concern. This paper provides an exhaustive comparative analysis of **water-cooled data centers** (open-loop systems) and **closed-loop cooling systems**, evaluating their technical, economic, and environmental implications. The findings are contextualized for the **City of Quinte West**, **Hastings County**, and the **Trent River / Bay of Quinte watershed**, with specific reference to proposed data center sites at **7 Riverside Drive** (former Saputo Dairy) and **920 Trenton Frankford Road** (former Sonoco Paper Mill).  

---

## Overview of Data Center Cooling Systems  

### 2.1 Water Cooling (Open-Loop Systems)  
Water-cooled data centers utilize **evaporative cooling** or **direct water cooling** to dissipate heat from IT equipment. In open-loop systems, water is drawn from municipal or natural sources (e.g., rivers, lakes), circulated through heat exchangers, and then discharged as waste water. This method is energy-efficient in hot climates but consumes significant volumes of water, often exceeding **6.45 million m³ annually** for a 200 MW IT load (Table 2, ^8).  

**Key Characteristics**:  
- **Water Consumption**: Direct water use (Scope 1) is tied to facility operations, while indirect water use (Scope 2) arises from electricity generation (e.g., thermal or nuclear plants using evaporative cooling) ^1.  
- **Energy Efficiency**: Water-cooled systems typically achieve **Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE)** values of 1.2–1.5, depending on climate and facility design ^6.  
- **Environmental Impact**: High water consumption can exacerbate local water scarcity, particularly in regions with limited renewable water supplies ^5.  

### 2.2 Closed-Loop Cooling Systems  
Closed-loop systems recirculate water through sealed pipes or coils, minimizing direct water withdrawal. These systems often employ **adiabatic cooling**, **direct-to-chip liquid cooling**, or **hybrid airside economizers** to reduce water use. By reusing water and only adding small amounts for makeup, closed-loop systems can cut water consumption by **up to 70%** compared to open-loop systems ^5.  

**Key Characteristics**:  
- **Water Consumption**: Direct water use is drastically reduced (e.g., **0.21 million m³ annually** for a 200 MW IT load in air-cooled configurations, ^8).  
- **Energy Efficiency**: Hybrid systems (e.g., airside economizers + water-cooled chillers) achieve lower PUE (e.g., **2.76 TWh facility electricity demand** for 200 MW IT load, ^8).  
- **Environmental Impact**: Reduced water consumption lowers the risk of depleting local water resources but increases reliance on **indirect water use** (Scope 2), which depends on regional electricity generation mix ^10.

## Comparative Analysis of Water and Energy Efficiency  

### 3.1 Water Consumption Metrics: WUE and Scope 1/2  
**Water Usage Effectiveness (WUE)**, a metric proposed by The Green Grid, quantifies water efficiency in liters per kilowatt-hour (L/kWh) of IT electricity ^1. Recent updates, such as **WUE+**, incorporate **regional water availability** and **reuse efficiency** to refine water footprint assessments ^6.  

**Comparative Data**:  
- **Water-cooled systems** (open-loop):  
  - **Direct water use**: 6.45 million m³/year (200 MW IT load, ^8).  
  - **Indirect water use**: 4.48 million m³/year (Scope 2, ^8).  
  - **Total water use**: 10.93 million m³/year (Table 2, ^8).  
- **Air-cooled systems** (closed-loop):  
  - **Direct water use**: 0.21 million m³/year (Table 2, ^8).  
  - **Indirect water use**: 5.91 million m³/year (Scope 2, ^8).  
  - **Total water use**: 6.12 million m³/year (Table 2, ^8).  
- **Hybrid systems** (closed-loop + airside economizer):  
  - **Direct water use**: 3.20 million m³/year (Table 2, ^8).  
  - **Indirect water use**: 3.98 million m³/year (Table 2, ^8).  
  - **Total water use**: 7.19 million m³/year (Table 2, ^8).  

**Key Insight**: While closed-loop systems reduce **direct water use**, they increase **indirect water use** (Scope 2), which is influenced by regional electricity generation (e.g., thermal/nuclear plants using evaporative cooling) ^10.  

### 3.2 Energy Consumption and PUE  
**Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE)** measures the ratio of total facility energy use to IT equipment energy use. Lower PUE values indicate greater energy efficiency.  

**Comparative Data**:  
- **Water-cooled systems**: PUE of 1.5–2.0 (Table 2, ^8).  
- **Air-cooled systems**: PUE of 2.5–3.0 (Table 2, ^8).  
- **Hybrid systems**: PUE of 1.2–1.5 (Table 2, ^8).  

**Key Insight**: Hybrid systems balance energy efficiency and water use, achieving **lower PUE** than air-cooled systems while reducing direct water consumption compared to water-cooled systems ^10.  

### 3.3 Environmental and Economic Impacts  
- **Water Scarcity**: Open-loop systems risk depleting local water resources, particularly in arid regions or watersheds with high demand (e.g., the Trent River / Bay of Quinte watershed, ^12).  
- **Cost**: Closed-loop systems require higher upfront capital investment (e.g., for sealed piping and heat exchangers) but reduce long-term water costs ^5.  
- **Carbon Footprint**: Indirect water use (Scope 2) is tied to electricity generation mix. Regions reliant on **coal or nuclear power** may see higher indirect water use, while **renewable energy** (e.g., wind, solar) reduces this impact ^1.

## Case Studies and Regional Implications  

### 4.1 Michigan Data Center Model: A Framework for Analysis  
A case study in **Michigan** models the environmental impact of data centers using an integrated **water–energy system**. The study evaluates three scenarios:  
1. **Water-cooled chillers**: High direct water use (70.71 million m³/year) but low indirect use (53.41 million m³/year, ^8).  
2. **Air-cooled chillers**: Low direct water use (0.21 million m³/year) but high indirect use (5.91 million m³/year, ^8).  
3. **Hybrid systems**: Balanced water use (7.19 million m³/year, ^8).  

**Key Insight**: The choice of cooling system shifts the water footprint between **on-site** (direct use) and **off-site** (indirect use) locations, depending on regional electricity generation mix ^10.  

### 4.2 Application to Quinte West and Hastings County  
The **Trent River / Bay of Quinte watershed** is characterized by **abundant freshwater resources** (Great Lakes proximity) and a **clean electricity grid** (Ontario’s renewable energy mix, ^12). However, proposed data center sites at **7 Riverside Drive** and **920 Trenton Frankford Road** must consider:  
- **Water Availability**: The Trent River’s flow and quality must be assessed to ensure open-loop systems do not strain local ecosystems.  
- **Energy Mix**: Ontario’s reliance on **nuclear and hydroelectric power** (Table 3, ^9) reduces indirect water use (Scope 2) compared to regions with coal-heavy grids.  
- **Climate**: Quinte West’s **moderate climate** (cooler winters, milder summers) may favor **hybrid systems** (airside economizers + water-cooled chillers) to balance energy efficiency and water use ^10.  

**Recommendation**: Given the region’s **high water availability** and **low indirect water use** from electricity generation, **closed-loop systems** (e.g., direct-to-chip liquid cooling) could minimize environmental impact while leveraging Ontario’s clean energy infrastructure ^12.

## Challenges and Considerations for Implementation  

### 5.1 Technical and Logistical Challenges  
- **Closed-loop systems** require **complex infrastructure** (e.g., sealed piping, heat exchangers) and **maintenance** to prevent leaks or corrosion ^5.  
- **Open-loop systems** depend on **reliable water sources** and may face regulatory hurdles in water-scarce regions ^1.  

### 5.2 Regulatory and Community Engagement  
- **Water Use Permits**: In Quinte West, data centers must comply with **Hastings County’s water management policies** and **Trent River conservation plans**.  
- **Community Acceptance**: Public concerns about **water pollution** or **energy demand** must be addressed through transparent planning and stakeholder engagement ^12.  

### 5.3 Future Trends and Innovations  
- **AI-Driven Cooling Optimization**: Machine learning algorithms can dynamically adjust cooling systems to reduce energy and water use ^1.  
- **Rainwater Harvesting**: Integrating **rainwater collection** (e.g., 1 million-gallon tanks) can offset up to **30% of water use** in data centers (Figure 14, ^3).  

---

## Conclusion  

The choice between **water-cooled** and **closed-loop cooling systems** for data centers hinges on a nuanced evaluation of **water availability**, **energy mix**, **climate**, and **economic factors**. While open-loop systems offer **lower upfront costs** and **high energy efficiency**, they risk **exacerbating water scarcity** in sensitive regions. Conversely, closed-loop systems reduce **direct water use** but increase **indirect water consumption**, which depends on regional electricity generation.  

For **Quinte West** and the **Trent River / Bay of Quinte watershed**, the **abundant freshwater supply** and **clean energy grid** make **closed-loop systems** a viable and environmentally responsible choice. However, **hybrid systems** may offer the optimal balance, leveraging **airside economizers** during cooler months and **water-cooled chillers** when necessary.  

Municipal planners, environmental agencies, and data center operators must collaborate to ensure that infrastructure development aligns with **sustainability goals**, **water conservation**, and **community well-being**. By adopting **WUE+** metrics and integrating **rainwater harvesting**, the region can lead in **green data center innovation** while safeguarding its natural resources.
