# Data Center Spills and Contamination in Asia: Environmental and Municipal Infrastructure Implications  

## Introduction  

Data centers, the backbone of global digital infrastructure, are increasingly scrutinized for their environmental and infrastructural impacts. As demand for cloud computing, artificial technical (AI), and big data analytics surges, the energy and water consumption of data centers has grown exponentially. This paper examines the technical, environmental, and municipal infrastructure challenges associated with data center operations, with a focus on water usage, contamination risks, and the potential implications for regions such as the Trent River / Bay of Quinte watershed in Quinte West, Hastings County, Ontario. While the raw evidence corpus primarily references studies from North America and Europe, the methodologies and risks identified are extrapolable to Asia, where data center expansion is accelerating.  

This analysis integrates findings from peer-reviewed studies, technical reports, and environmental impact assessments to evaluate the risks of water contamination, thermal runaway in battery energy storage systems (BESS), and the trade-offs between cooling technologies and water sustainability. The paper concludes with recommendations for mitigating these risks in municipal infrastructure planning, particularly for proposed data center sites in Quinte West and Hastings County.

## Data Center Cooling Systems and Water Usage  

### Evaporative Cooling and Water Consumption  

Data centers rely heavily on cooling systems to dissipate heat generated by servers and IT equipment. Evaporative cooling, a common method in hot climates, uses water to absorb heat through evaporation. However, this process consumes significant volumes of water, often sourced from municipal supplies. According to the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA, 2026a), data centers’ water demand is increasing rapidly alongside the growth of cloud computing and AI services ^1.  

Evaporative cooling systems typically operate in open-loop configurations, where water is drawn from a municipal supply, evaporated to cool the air, and then discharged as wastewater. This method is energy-efficient but results in high water consumption. For example, a large data center using evaporative cooling may withdraw millions of gallons of water annually, with a portion lost to evaporation and blowdown (periodic discharge to control mineral buildup) ^11.  

### Closed-Loop Water Cooling and Sustainability  

In contrast, closed-loop water cooling systems recirculate water through sealed pipes or coils, reducing direct water consumption by up to 70% compared to open-loop systems ^1. These systems are more expensive and complex but offer significant water savings, making them a preferred choice for data centers in water-stressed regions. However, the increased energy consumption required to maintain closed-loop systems may offset some of the environmental benefits, depending on the energy mix of the local grid ^2.  

### Implications for the Trent River Watershed  

The Trent River / Bay of Quinte watershed, which flows through Quinte West and Hastings County, is a critical ecological and municipal resource. If a data center were to be established at the proposed site on 7 Riverside Drive (former Saputo Dairy), the use of evaporative cooling could strain local water resources. Even with closed-loop systems, the potential for water contamination from chemical leaks or thermal runaway in BESS (discussed below) would pose risks to the Trent River’s water quality and aquatic ecosystems.

## Water Management Strategies and Environmental Risks  

### Rainwater Harvesting and Stress-Adjusted Water Use  

To mitigate water consumption, some data centers are adopting rainwater harvesting systems. Studies have shown that in suitable regions, rainwater harvesting can offset up to 100% of on-site cooling water demand ^2. For example, a 1,000,000-gallon rainwater harvesting system paired with a 1,000,000 sqft harvesting area could reduce water stress by up to 80% in certain locations ^7.  

However, the feasibility of rainwater harvesting depends on local precipitation patterns and storage capacity. In regions with low annual rainfall, such as parts of Asia, this strategy may be less effective. For the Trent River watershed, which experiences moderate rainfall, rainwater harvesting could complement municipal water supplies but would require careful planning to avoid over-reliance on seasonal precipitation.  

### Dry Cooling and Energy-Water Trade-Offs  

Dry cooling technologies eliminate on-site water use entirely by relying on air instead of water for heat dissipation. While this reduces direct water withdrawal, it increases electricity consumption, which in turn shifts water consumption to power plants. This creates a trade-off between on-site water savings and indirect water use from energy generation ^2.  

For example, if a data center in Quinte West uses dry cooling, the increased electricity demand could lead to higher water consumption at power plants, potentially exacerbating water stress in regions where the grid relies on water-intensive cooling methods. This underscores the need for integrated water-energy planning in municipal infrastructure.

## Environmental and Health Risks: Contamination and Thermal Runaway  

### Chemical Contamination from Cooling Systems  

Data centers use various chemicals in their cooling systems, including propylene glycol, which is sometimes used as an antifreeze agent in closed-loop systems. Propylene glycol is generally considered less toxic than ethylene glycol, but it can still pose risks to aquatic life and human health if released into waterways ^5.  

For instance, a spill of propylene glycol from a data center’s cooling system at the proposed 920 Trenton Frankford Road site (former Sonoco Paper Mill) could contaminate the Trent River, affecting fish populations and potentially entering the municipal water supply. The EPA’s Toxicological Profile for propylene glycol highlights the importance of proper containment and spill response protocols to mitigate such risks ^5.  

### Thermal Runaway in Battery Energy Storage Systems (BESS)  

Data centers increasingly rely on BESS to store renewable energy and manage peak loads. However, thermal runaway in lithium-ion batteries—a rapid, uncontrolled rise in temperature—can lead to fires, explosions, and the release of toxic gases ^3.  

Studies have shown that thermal runaway can be triggered by mechanical abuse, overcharging, or internal short circuits. Early warning systems using gas sensors and machine learning algorithms can detect thermal runaway up to 14.8 frames (approximately 0.2 seconds) before it occurs, providing critical time for mitigation ^4.  

If a BESS at a data center in Quinte West were to experience thermal runaway, the resulting fire could release toxic gases such as hydrogen fluoride and carbon monoxide, posing immediate health risks to nearby residents and workers. Additionally, the contamination of soil and groundwater from battery electrolytes would require extensive remediation efforts.

## Case Study: Potential Impacts on the Trent River Watershed  

### Scenario: Data Center at 7 Riverside Drive (Former Saputo Dairy)  

If a data center were to be established at 7 Riverside Drive, the use of evaporative cooling could increase water withdrawals from the Trent River, potentially reducing flow rates and affecting aquatic habitats. Additionally, the discharge of wastewater containing propylene glycol or other cooling chemicals could lead to contamination of the river, with long-term ecological consequences.  

To mitigate these risks, the data center would need to implement closed-loop cooling systems, rainwater harvesting, and advanced spill containment measures. The Trent River’s low water stress levels (compared to regions like Arizona or Rajasthan) make it a relatively suitable location, but careful monitoring of water quality and ecosystem health would be essential.  

### Scenario: Data Center at 920 Trenton Frankford Road (Former Sonoco Paper Mill)  

A data center at the former Sonoco Paper Mill site would face similar challenges, particularly if BESS are used. The risk of thermal runaway in batteries necessitates robust fire suppression systems and emergency response protocols. Additionally, the proximity to the Trent River requires stringent measures to prevent chemical spills from reaching the waterway.  

The use of dry cooling could reduce on-site water use but would increase reliance on the local grid, which may still depend on water-intensive power generation methods. This highlights the need for a transition to renewable energy sources and grid decarbonization to achieve true sustainability.

## Recommendations for Sustainable Data Center Development  

1. **Adopt Closed-Loop Cooling Systems**: To minimize water consumption and reduce the risk of contamination, data centers should prioritize closed-loop systems over evaporative cooling, especially in ecologically sensitive areas like the Trent River watershed.  

2. **Implement Rainwater Harvesting**: Where feasible, rainwater harvesting should be integrated into data center designs to reduce reliance on municipal water supplies and mitigate the impacts of droughts or water shortages.  

3. **Deploy Advanced BESS Safety Protocols**: To prevent thermal runaway, data centers must use BESS with built-in early warning systems, gas sensors, and fire suppression technologies. Regular maintenance and staff training are also critical.  

4. **Conduct Environmental Impact Assessments (EIAs)**: Before approving new data center developments, municipal authorities in Quinte West and Hastings County should require comprehensive EIAs that assess risks to water quality, biodiversity, and public health.  

5. **Promote Renewable Energy Integration**: Reducing the carbon footprint of data centers through renewable energy procurement and grid decarbonization will help address both energy and water sustainability challenges.

## Conclusion  

Data centers are essential to the digital economy but pose significant environmental and infrastructural challenges, particularly in terms of water usage and contamination risks. The evidence corpus highlights the trade-offs between different cooling technologies, the potential for chemical spills, and the risks of thermal runaway in BESS. While the raw data primarily references North American and European contexts, the principles and risks identified are applicable to Asia, where data center expansion is accelerating.  

For regions like the Trent River / Bay of Quinte watershed, the proposed data center sites at 7 Riverside Drive and 920 Trenton Frankford Road must be evaluated with a focus on water sustainability, chemical safety, and long-term ecological health. By adopting closed-loop cooling, rainwater harvesting, and advanced BESS safety measures, municipalities can mitigate the environmental impacts of data centers while supporting economic growth.  

The integration of water-energy planning, renewable energy adoption, and rigorous environmental oversight will be critical to ensuring that data centers contribute to a sustainable future rather than exacerbating existing environmental challenges.
