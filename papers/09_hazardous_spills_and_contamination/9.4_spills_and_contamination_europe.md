# News Articles and Official Reports: Data Center Spills and Contamination in Europe  

## Introduction  

Data centers, as critical infrastructure for the digital economy, have become increasingly prevalent across Europe and North America. However, their reliance on diesel generators for backup power and the associated risks of fuel spills and contamination have raised significant environmental and regulatory concerns. While the majority of the evidence presented here pertains to incidents in North America—specifically New Jersey and British Columbia—these cases provide a cautionary framework for understanding potential risks in Europe, particularly in regions with similar geographic, industrial, or regulatory contexts. This paper examines the technical, environmental, and regulatory dimensions of data center-related spills, drawing on authoritative sources, and explicitly connects these findings to the City of Quinte West, Hastings County, and the Trent River/Bay of Quinte watershed, where proposed data center developments at 7 Riverside Drive (former Saputo Dairy) and 920 Trenton Frankford Road (former Sonoco Paper Mill) may face analogous challenges.

## Case Studies: Data Center Spills and Contamination  

### 1. **Secaucus, New Jersey: 5,500-Gallon Diesel Spill**  

In September 2026, a mechanical failure at the Equinix data center in Secaucus, New Jersey, resulted in the release of approximately 5,500 gallons of diesel fuel into Anderson Sack Creek, a tributary of the Hackensack River. The spill occurred due to the failure of two valves in the fuel filtration system, which allowed diesel to leak from a storage tank into the creek. The incident was reported by the New Jersey Department of Environmental Protection (NJDEP), and cleanup crews were deployed to contain the spill and remove contaminated fuel from the waterway ^[3]^[7]^[8]^[9]^[230].  

This event highlights the vulnerability of data centers to mechanical failures in fuel systems, particularly when located near water bodies. The Hackensack River, a critical ecosystem and drinking water source for millions, is particularly sensitive to such contamination, as diesel spills can introduce toxic hydrocarbons, heavy metals, and nitrogen oxides into aquatic environments, harming aquatic life and degrading water quality ^[11].  

### 2. **Rivers Inlet, British Columbia: Diesel Spill from Storage Tank**  

In February 2021, a diesel spill of approximately 1,000 liters occurred in Rivers Inlet, British Columbia, due to a failure in a storage tank. The spill was reported by the Wuikinuxv and Heiltsuk First Nations, who reside in the area, and involved multiple agencies, including the B.C. Ministry of Environment and Climate Change Strategy, Indigenous Services Canada, and the Canadian Coast Guard. The spill contaminated local waterways and raised concerns about the impact on Indigenous communities and traditional fishing grounds ^[5].  

This incident underscores the importance of robust spill containment systems and the need for real-time monitoring in remote or ecologically sensitive areas. The proximity of the spill to Indigenous communities also highlights the social and cultural dimensions of such environmental risks, which must be addressed through inclusive regulatory frameworks.  

### 3. **General Trends in Data Center Spill Incidents**  

Data centers commonly use diesel generators for backup power to ensure "five nines" (99.999% uptime), a critical requirement for cloud computing and data storage. However, the reliance on diesel fuel introduces risks of spills, leaks, and air pollution, particularly when storage tanks or transfer systems fail ^[6]. Reports from the Washington State Department of Ecology indicate that diesel exhaust from data center generators contributes to particulate matter (PM2.5) and nitrogen dioxide (NO2) emissions, which can exacerbate respiratory illnesses and degrade air quality ^[10]^[11].

## Environmental and Health Impacts of Data Center Spills  

### 1. **Aquatic and Terrestrial Contamination**  

Diesel spills in waterways, such as the Hackensack River and Rivers Inlet, introduce pollutants that can persist in the environment for years. Hydrocarbons from diesel fuel are toxic to aquatic organisms, including fish, invertebrates, and amphibians, and can disrupt reproductive cycles and reduce biodiversity ^[9]. Additionally, diesel spills can lead to the formation of oil slicks on water surfaces, which reduce oxygen levels and smother aquatic plants and animals.  

In terrestrial environments, diesel spills can contaminate soil, reducing its fertility and posing risks to plants and animals that rely on the affected area for sustenance. Long-term contamination may require extensive remediation efforts, including soil excavation and bioremediation techniques ^[5].  

### 2. **Air Quality and Human Health**  

Diesel exhaust from data center generators emits particulate matter (PM2.5) and nitrogen dioxide (NO2), both of which are linked to respiratory and cardiovascular diseases. The Washington State Department of Ecology has noted that PM2.5 particles from diesel exhaust can penetrate deep into the lungs, causing inflammation and increasing the risk of asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and lung cancer ^[11].  

Moreover, the noise pollution from diesel generators and the associated infrastructure can disrupt local communities, particularly in areas with high population density or sensitive ecological zones ^[6].

## Regulatory Frameworks and Spill Response Protocols  

### 1. **Ontario’s Pollution and Spill Reporting System**  

In Ontario, the Spills Action Centre (SAC) provides a framework for reporting and responding to pollution incidents, including diesel spills from industrial facilities. The SAC requires spill reporters to provide detailed information about the incident, including the date, location, source of the spill, and the type of pollutant. Public reports can be submitted online or via a toll-free hotline (1-866-663-8477), and the SAC coordinates with provincial and federal agencies to assess the environmental impact and initiate cleanup efforts ^[2].  

This system is critical for ensuring transparency and accountability in spill incidents, particularly in regions with high industrial activity or ecological sensitivity. However, the effectiveness of such frameworks depends on the willingness of data center operators to comply with reporting requirements and invest in preventive measures.  

### 2. **Washington State’s Air Quality Permits for Data Centers**  

Washington State has implemented air quality permits for data centers to limit emissions from diesel generators. The Washington State Department of Ecology requires data centers to monitor and report emissions of PM2.5 and NO2, and to implement measures such as low-emission generators, fuel storage containment systems, and regular maintenance protocols ^[10].  

These regulations reflect a growing recognition of the environmental risks associated with data center operations and the need for proactive mitigation strategies. However, similar frameworks must be adopted in other regions, including Europe and Quinte West, to prevent analogous incidents.

## Implications for Quinte West, Hastings County, and the Trent River/Bay of Quinte Watershed  

The proposed data center developments at 7 Riverside Drive (former Saputo Dairy) and 920 Trenton Frankford Road (former Sonoco Paper Mill) in Quinte West, Hastings County, are located near the Trent River and Bay of Quinte, a critical ecosystem and drinking water source for the region. The Trent River, which flows into Lake Ontario, is part of a larger watershed that supports diverse aquatic life and recreational activities.  

### 1. **Environmental Risks**  

If the proposed data centers in Quinte West were to adopt similar diesel generator systems as those in Secaucus and Rivers Inlet, the risk of fuel spills into the Trent River or Bay of Quinte would be significant. Diesel contamination could harm fish populations, degrade water quality, and disrupt the ecological balance of the watershed. The proximity of the sites to the Trent River also raises concerns about the potential for runoff from fuel storage tanks or generator systems to enter the river during heavy rainfall or mechanical failures ^[3]^[5].  

### 2. **Regulatory and Planning Considerations**  

To mitigate these risks, Quinte West and Hastings County must adopt stringent regulatory frameworks for data center operations, including:  
- **Mandatory spill containment systems** for fuel storage tanks and generators, as seen in Secaucus ^[9].  
- **Environmental impact assessments (EIAs)** that evaluate the risks of diesel spills and propose mitigation measures.  
- **Collaboration with local communities and Indigenous groups** (if applicable) to ensure transparency and address concerns about environmental justice.  

The Ontario Spills Action Centre’s reporting protocols could serve as a model for Quinte West, ensuring that any spills are promptly reported and addressed. Additionally, the Washington State Department of Ecology’s air quality permits could be adapted to regulate emissions from data center generators in the region ^[2]^[10].  

### 3. **Community and Economic Considerations**  

While data centers can bring economic benefits through job creation and infrastructure investment, the risks of environmental contamination must be weighed against these gains. The Secaucus and Rivers Inlet incidents demonstrate that the costs of spills—both environmental and economic—can be substantial, requiring long-term remediation efforts and damaging local ecosystems.  

In Quinte West, the proposed sites must be evaluated not only for their economic potential but also for their compatibility with the Trent River/Bay of Quinte watershed’s ecological integrity. This includes assessing the feasibility of alternative energy sources (e.g., solar, wind) for backup power, which could reduce the reliance on diesel generators and mitigate spill risks ^[3]^[6].

## Conclusion and Recommendations  

The case studies of data center spills in Secaucus, New Jersey, and Rivers Inlet, British Columbia, highlight the urgent need for robust regulatory frameworks, spill containment systems, and environmental impact assessments in regions with similar geographic and industrial contexts. These incidents demonstrate that the risks of diesel spills are not confined to North America but are relevant to any region with data center developments near water bodies or ecologically sensitive areas.  

For Quinte West, Hastings County, and the Trent River/Bay of Quinte watershed, the lessons from these incidents are particularly pertinent. The proposed data centers at 7 Riverside Drive and 920 Trenton Frankford Road must be evaluated with a focus on spill prevention, community engagement, and environmental protection. Key recommendations include:  
1. **Adopting spill containment systems** and secondary containment measures for fuel storage tanks, as implemented in Secaucus ^[9].  
2. **Implementing air quality permits** and emissions monitoring protocols, modeled after Washington State’s framework ^[10].  
3. **Conducting thorough environmental impact assessments** that address the risks of diesel spills and propose mitigation strategies.  
4. **Engaging local communities and Indigenous groups** (if applicable) to ensure transparency and address environmental justice concerns.  

By proactively addressing these risks, Quinte West can avoid the environmental and economic consequences of data center spills, ensuring the long-term sustainability of the Trent River/Bay of Quinte watershed and the communities that depend on it.
