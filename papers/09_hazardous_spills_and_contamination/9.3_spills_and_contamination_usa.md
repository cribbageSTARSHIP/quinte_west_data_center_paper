# Data Center Spills and Contamination in the USA: Environmental, Health, and Regulatory Implications  

## Introduction  

The rapid expansion of data centers in the United States has introduced new environmental and public health challenges, particularly concerning spills of hazardous substances such as diesel fuel, ethylene glycol, and other industrial chemicals. These incidents, often linked to the construction and operation of data centers, pose significant risks to water quality, ecosystems, and human health. This paper examines documented cases of data center-related spills, their environmental and health impacts, and the regulatory frameworks governing such incidents. Special attention is given to the potential implications for the City of Quinte West, Hastings County, and the Trent River / Bay of Quinte watershed, particularly in relation to proposed data center sites at 7 Riverside Drive (former Saputo Dairy) and 920 Trenton Frankford Road (former Sonoco Paper Mill).  

---

## Case Study: The 5,500-Gallon Diesel Spill in Secaucus, New Jersey  

### Incident Overview  

On Friday night, a significant diesel fuel spill occurred at the Equinix data center under construction on Hartz Way in Secaucus, New Jersey. Approximately **5,500 gallons of diesel** spilled from a storage tank into **Anderson Sack Creek**, a tributary of the **Hackensack River**. This event highlights the risks associated with the storage and handling of large volumes of fuel at data centers, particularly those located near water bodies ^2.  

### Environmental Impact  

Diesel fuel contains toxic hydrocarbons, including polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), which are known to contaminate waterways and harm aquatic life. The spill into Anderson Sack Creek, which flows into the Hackensack River, raises concerns about the long-term ecological damage to the region. The Hackensack River is a critical waterway for both recreational and commercial activities, and contamination could disrupt local fisheries and biodiversity ^2.  

### Regulatory and Mitigation Measures  

Following the spill, local authorities and the **U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)** would likely initiate cleanup efforts under the **Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act (CERCLA)**. Such incidents also underscore the need for stringent permitting and spill prevention protocols, particularly for data centers located in ecologically sensitive areas ^9.  

---

## Health Implications of Data Center Spills  

### Toxicological Risks of Spill-Related Chemicals  

Data center spills often involve substances such as **ethylene glycol** and **propylene glycol**, which are used in cooling systems and fire suppression technologies. Ethylene glycol, in particular, is highly toxic to humans and animals, with acute exposure leading to kidney failure, central nervous system depression, and even death ^1. Chronic exposure to low concentrations of these chemicals can result in long-term health effects, including respiratory issues, liver damage, and increased cancer risk ^3.  

### Air and Water Quality Concerns  

Spills of diesel fuel and other hydrocarbons can release volatile organic compounds (VOCs) into the air, contributing to poor air quality and exacerbating respiratory conditions such as asthma. The **World Health Organization (WHO)** has linked ambient air pollution to **7 million premature deaths annually**, with VOCs playing a significant role in this burden ^8. In regions like the **Trent River / Bay of Quinte watershed**, where water quality is already sensitive to industrial activity, such spills could further degrade aquatic ecosystems and threaten drinking water sources ^3.  

### Public Health and Community Impact  

The **Health-Informed Computing** study highlights the broader public health implications of data center expansion, including increased energy consumption, air pollution, and the potential for chemical spills ^5. In communities near data centers, such as those in **Quinte West** and **Hastings County**, residents may face heightened risks of exposure to contaminants if spill prevention measures are not strictly enforced ^7.  

---

## Regulatory Frameworks and Compliance Challenges  

### Clean Air Act and EPA Guidelines  

The **Clean Air Act (CAA)** provides a legal framework for regulating emissions and spills from industrial facilities, including data centers. Under the CAA, the **EPA** has issued guidelines for air quality modeling, permitting, and compliance with **National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS)** ^9. These guidelines are critical for ensuring that data centers do not contribute to exceedances of pollutant thresholds, particularly in areas with existing air quality issues ^10.  

### Permitting and Environmental Assessments  

Data centers require **air permits** under the **New Source Review (NSR)** program, which mandates that facilities demonstrate compliance with air quality standards before construction ^10. However, the **EPA’s** recent focus on reducing regulatory burdens, such as the **repeal of 2024 power plant regulations**, may inadvertently weaken protections against spills and emissions ^9. This raises concerns about the adequacy of current permitting processes in preventing incidents like the Secaucus diesel spill.  

### Local and State-Level Regulations  

In addition to federal regulations, **state and local authorities** play a crucial role in enforcing environmental protections. For example, **Virginia’s** **Department of Environmental Quality (DEQ)** issues air permits for data centers, ensuring that facilities adhere to state-specific guidelines ^7. In **Ontario**, where the **City of Quinte West** and **Hastings County** are located, similar regulations would be necessary to mitigate risks at the proposed data center sites.

## Case Study: Health Impacts in Virginia and Implications for Quinte West  

### Virginia’s Data Center Expansion and Public Health  

A 2023 study on the **health implications of data center expansion in Virginia** found that increased energy consumption and associated emissions could exacerbate air pollution, leading to higher rates of respiratory and cardiovascular diseases ^3. The study also highlighted the need for **zero-emission power generation** to mitigate these risks ^3.  

### Relevance to Quinte West  

If data centers are constructed at the proposed sites in **Quinte West**, similar public health risks could emerge, particularly if the facilities rely on diesel generators or other fossil fuel-based energy sources. The **Trent River**’s proximity to these sites increases the likelihood of water contamination from spills, which could affect both human populations and the **Bay of Quinte** ecosystem ^7.  

---

## Mitigation Strategies and Recommendations  

### Engineering and Operational Safeguards  

To prevent spills, data centers should adopt **closed-loop cooling systems**, redundant fuel storage tanks, and real-time monitoring technologies. The **EPA’s** **COBRA (Co-benefits Risk Assessment)** tool can help quantify the health benefits of such measures, providing a cost-benefit analysis for facility operators ^18.  

### Community Engagement and Transparency  

Data centers must engage with local communities to address concerns about environmental risks. In **Quinte West**, this could involve public consultations on spill prevention plans and emergency response protocols for the **Trent River** watershed.  

### Strengthening Regulatory Frameworks  

Federal and state regulators should reinforce permitting requirements for data centers, ensuring that facilities in ecologically sensitive areas (like the **Bay of Quinte**) meet the highest standards for spill prevention and environmental protection. The **EPA’s** **Guideline on Air Quality Models** should be updated to reflect the unique challenges posed by data center operations ^10.  

---

## Conclusion  

The **Secaucus diesel spill** and other documented incidents underscore the urgent need for robust environmental safeguards in the data center industry. As **Quinte West** and **Hastings County** consider proposals for new data centers, lessons from past spills must inform regulatory decisions to protect the **Trent River / Bay of Quinte** watershed and public health. By integrating advanced engineering solutions, community engagement, and strengthened regulations, the risks associated with data center operations can be minimized, ensuring sustainable development in the digital age.
