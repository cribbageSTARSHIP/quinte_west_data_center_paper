# Data Center Spills and Contamination in Canada: A Comprehensive Analysis of Environmental and Regulatory Challenges in Alberta and Beyond (Excluding Ontario)

---

## Introduction

Data centers, as the backbone of modern digital infrastructure, have become focal points of environmental and regulatory scrutiny due to their significant energy and water consumption, as well as their potential to cause contamination through operational spills or improper waste management. While much of the discourse on data center impacts has centered on Ontario, this paper examines the situation in **Alberta**, Canada, where rapid expansion of artificial technical (AI) and hyperscale data centers has raised critical concerns about environmental degradation, water resource depletion, and regulatory oversight. Drawing on authoritative raw evidence from public reports, academic studies, and environmental advocacy documents, this paper analyzes the technical, legal, and ecological dimensions of data center operations in Alberta, with implications for other regions in Canada (excluding Ontario). The analysis also explicitly connects findings to the **City of Quinte West**, **Hastings County**, and the **Trent River / Bay of Quinte watershed**, highlighting potential parallels in infrastructure planning and environmental risk management for proposed sites such as **7 Riverside Drive** (former Saputo Dairy) and **920 Trenton Frankford Road** (former Sonoco Paper Mill).

## Environmental Impacts of Data Centers: Water Use, Contamination, and Cooling Systems

### 1. Water Consumption and Contamination Risks

Data centers are among the most water-intensive industrial facilities in the world, primarily due to their reliance on **evaporative cooling systems** for heat dissipation. According to the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA, 2026a), modern data centers consume **vast volumes of water**, often drawn from municipal supplies, to cool servers and maintain operational temperatures. In Alberta, where large-scale AI data centers are being proposed, this demand is projected to escalate dramatically. For instance, the **Synapse data center complex** in Olds, Alberta, which would be powered by a **1.4 gigawatt natural gas power plant**, is estimated to consume **5.6 billion liters of water annually**—equivalent to the annual use of **79,000 Albertans** (Source 7). If all proposed data centers in Alberta are realized, their power plants alone could consume **up to 1.6 million Albertans’ worth of water annually** (Source 7). This level of consumption raises concerns about **local water scarcity**, particularly in drought-prone regions, and the **contamination of wastewater** generated through evaporative cooling processes, which often contain high concentrations of **heavy metals, salts, and other contaminants** (Source 7).

### 2. Cooling System Technologies and Their Trade-Offs

To mitigate water consumption, some data centers employ **closed-loop water cooling systems**, which recirculate water through sealed pipes or coils, reducing direct water withdrawal by up to **70%** compared to open-loop evaporative cooling (Source 2). However, these systems are **more expensive** and require **greater technical complexity**, which may limit their adoption in regions with limited infrastructure or regulatory incentives. In Alberta, the **Alberta government** has promoted the use of **rainwater harvesting** as a mitigation strategy, with studies suggesting that in suitable regions, it could offset **100% of on-site cooling water demand** (Source 10). Conversely, **dry-cooling technologies**, which eliminate on-site water use entirely, shift water consumption to **power plants**, where water stress may be significantly higher (Source 10). This trade-off underscores the need for **stress-adjusted water management strategies** that balance **water and carbon objectives** (Source 10).

### 3. Contamination Risks from Spills and Wastewater

Beyond water consumption, data centers pose **contamination risks** through **spills of cooling fluids**, **wastewater discharge**, and **chemical leaks** from power generation infrastructure. For example, the **Meta**-proposed **970-MW data center** in **Sturgeon County, Alberta**, has drawn criticism for its reliance on **fossil fuels** for power generation, which could exacerbate **carbon emissions** and **water pollution** (Source 8). Additionally, the **Alberta Wilderness Association** (AWA) has highlighted that hyperscale AI data centers can **reduce air and water quality**, **increase heat exposure**, and **disrupt ecosystems** through **noise pollution** and **land use changes** (Source 9). These risks are compounded by the lack of **mandatory environmental assessments** for many data center projects, as noted in **Alberta’s regulatory framework**, which has been criticized for **insufficient public consultation** and **transparency** (Source 4).

## Regulatory and Legal Frameworks in Alberta

### 1. Provincial and Federal Policy Context

Alberta has positioned itself as a **global hub for AI and data center development**, supported by **federal and provincial policy changes** aimed at facilitating infrastructure expansion. The **November 2025 Memorandum of Understanding (MOU)** between Canada and Alberta outlines goals to **increase electrical generation** for AI data centers while achieving **net-zero greenhouse gas emissions** in the electricity sector by **2050** (Source 11). However, this vision has been met with **local opposition** and **regulatory challenges**, as seen in the **Alberta Utilities Commission’s rejection** of the **Synapse data center’s proposed natural gas power plant** in **Olds** (Source 12). The commission cited **safety concerns** and **grid capacity limitations**, emphasizing that the project’s **1.4 gigawatt power generation** would **exceed the grid’s safe load capacity** (Source 12).

### 2. Zoning and Community Opposition

Municipal **zoning approvals** play a critical role in data center development, as highlighted in **Alberta’s regulatory framework**. For example, **Rocky View County** rejected a **1,100-acre data center campus** in **September 2025** due to **zoning and land use concerns** (Source 3). Similarly, the **Alberta Utilities Commission** closed a data center power plant application in **March 2026** for **failing to meet filing requirements**, demonstrating the **independent regulatory checks** in place (Source 3). These examples illustrate the **power of local governance** in shaping data center projects, even in the face of **provincial and federal support** for AI infrastructure.

### 3. Legal and Environmental Advocacy

Environmental organizations, such as the **Alberta Wilderness Association** (AWA), have actively opposed data center expansions, citing **ecological harm** and **insufficient public consultation** (Source 9). In **December 2024**, a joint letter from AWA and other groups called for a **moratorium on AI hyperscale data center development**, emphasizing the **lack of transparency** and **unaddressed environmental impacts** (Source 9). These efforts have influenced **regulatory decisions**, such as the **Alberta Utilities Commission’s rejection** of the **Synapse project**, which was partly attributed to **community opposition** (Source 12).

## Case Study: The Synapse Data Center and Its Environmental and Regulatory Implications

### 1. Project Overview and Controversies

The **Synapse data center complex** in **Olds, Alberta**, proposed by **Synapse Real Estate Corp.**, is one of the most contentious data center projects in Canada. The plan includes a **10-building campus** with **1.4 gigawatts of power generation** and **1.8 gigawatts of emergency diesel backup**, sufficient to power **Edmonton** (Source 12). However, the **Alberta Utilities Commission** rejected the project in **2026**, citing **safety concerns** and **grid capacity limitations** (Source 12). The **Town of Olds** had initially expressed interest in the project, estimating **millions in property tax revenue** from the data center (Source 6). However, **residents and environmental groups** raised concerns about **water use**, **carbon emissions**, and **local opposition** (Source 12).

### 2. Environmental and Water Resource Concerns

The Synapse project’s **1.4 gigawatt power plant** would require **massive water consumption** for cooling, potentially exacerbating **local water scarcity** in **Alberta** (Source 7). Additionally, the **reliance on natural gas** and **diesel backup** raises **carbon emission concerns**, aligning with broader criticisms of **fossil fuel dependency** in data center infrastructure (Source 8). The **Alberta Wilderness Association** has highlighted that such projects could **disrupt ecosystems**, **increase heat exposure**, and **contaminate water sources** (Source 9).

### 3. Regulatory and Community Responses

The **Alberta Utilities Commission’s rejection** of the Synapse project underscores the **importance of independent regulatory oversight** in data center development (Source 12). The decision also reflects **community opposition**, as **residents** in **Olds** voiced **fervent concerns** about the project’s **environmental and safety risks** (Source 12). This case illustrates the **tension between economic incentives** (e.g., **tax revenue**) and **environmental protection**, a dynamic that could mirror **proposed developments in Quinte West** or **Hastings County** if similar projects were pursued.

## Implications for the Trent River / Bay of Quinte Watershed and Proposed Sites in Quinte West

While the focus of this paper is on Alberta, the **Trent River / Bay of Quinte watershed** and proposed data center sites in **Quinte West** and **Hastings County** (e.g., **7 Riverside Drive** and **920 Trenton Frankford Road**) present **parallel risks** and **regulatory considerations**. For instance:

- **Water Use**: If data centers were developed in Quinte West using **evaporative cooling**, they could **exacerbate local water scarcity** in the **Trent River watershed**, similar to concerns in Alberta (Source 7).
- **Contamination Risks**: Spills from **cooling systems** or **power generation infrastructure** at the **former Saputo Dairy** or **Sonoco Paper Mill** sites could **contaminate groundwater** or **surface water** in the **Bay of Quinte**, mirroring the **wastewater contamination risks** observed in Alberta (Source 7).
- **Regulatory Framework**: Unlike Alberta, where **municipal councils** and **independent regulators** have **rejected projects**, **Quinte West** and **Hastings County** would need to ensure **rigorous environmental assessments** and **public consultation** to avoid **repeating Alberta’s controversies** (Source 3).

## Conclusion and Recommendations

The expansion of data centers in Alberta has exposed **critical environmental and regulatory challenges**, including **excessive water consumption**, **contamination risks**, and **insufficient public consultation**. While the province has **attracted global investment** through **competitive regulatory frameworks**, the **rejection of projects like Synapse** highlights the **need for stronger environmental safeguards**. For regions like **Quinte West** and **Hastings County**, these lessons underscore the importance of **integrating sustainable cooling technologies**, **mandating environmental assessments**, and **prioritizing community input** in infrastructure planning. Future developments must balance **economic incentives** with **ecological responsibility**, ensuring that data centers do not become **sources of contamination** or **water depletion** in the **Trent River / Bay of Quinte watershed** or elsewhere in Canada.
