## How Governments Around the World Have Dealt with Accidents Involving Data Centers  

### Introduction  

Data centers, as critical infrastructure for global digital economies, are increasingly recognized as high-risk facilities due to their reliance on energy-intensive operations, backup power systems, and complex cooling mechanisms. Accidents involving data centers—ranging from chemical spills to power outages—pose significant environmental, public health, and economic risks. Governments worldwide have responded with a mix of regulatory frameworks, emergency protocols, and technological safeguards. This paper examines these responses, drawing on authoritative evidence from U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) documents, technical studies, and real-world case studies. Special attention is given to the implications for the City of Quinte West, Hastings County, and the Trent River/Bay of Quinte watershed, particularly in relation to proposed data center sites at 7 Riverside Drive (former Saputo Dairy) and 920 Trenton Frankford Road (former Sonoco Paper Mill).  

---

### Global Overview of Data Center Accident Management  

Data center accidents are not confined to any single region. However, the U.S. EPA’s Clean Air Act Resources for Data Centers (2026) and related studies provide a detailed framework for understanding how governments have addressed such incidents, particularly in the context of air quality, emissions, and spill containment. These materials highlight a focus on **prevention, emergency response, and regulatory compliance**, with lessons applicable to other jurisdictions.  

#### 1. **Regulatory Frameworks and Permitting**  

The U.S. Clean Air Act (CAA) has been central to managing data center-related risks. Key provisions include:  
- **New Source Review (NSR)**: Requires permits for new or modified sources of air pollution, including data centers with backup generators or cooling systems.  
- **Title V Operating Permits**: Mandate compliance with emission standards for facilities that meet or exceed thresholds for hazardous pollutants.  
- **Emission Standards for Stationary Sources**: Apply to generators, combustion engines, and other equipment used in data centers.  

For example, the EPA’s guidance on **Potential to Emit (PTE)** for emergency generators assumes a default of 500 hours of operation under worst-case conditions, though this can be adjusted based on historical data (e.g., local power outage frequencies) ^12. This approach ensures that permits reflect realistic scenarios while avoiding overestimation of risks.  

In the context of the Trent River watershed, similar permitting requirements would apply to proposed data centers at 7 Riverside Drive and 920 Trenton Frankford Road. These sites would need to demonstrate compliance with provincial and federal regulations, including spill containment measures and air quality standards, to mitigate risks to the Bay of Quinte ecosystem.  

#### 2. **Emergency Response and Power Management**  

The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) has issued emergency orders during periods of extreme heat, requiring data centers to activate backup generators to prevent load interruptions that could harm public health ^4. These orders, issued under the Federal Power Act Section 202(c), highlight the intersection of energy policy and environmental protection.  

While such measures are specific to the U.S., similar principles could be applied in Quinte West, where extreme weather events (e.g., heatwaves or storms) might strain the local power grid. The proposed data centers would need to integrate **redundant power systems** and **spill containment infrastructure** to avoid disruptions akin to the 2026 Equinix diesel spill in New Jersey ^9.  

#### 3. **Spill Containment and Environmental Protection**  

Data centers often store diesel fuel for backup generators, posing risks of spills into waterways or soil. The EPA’s Clean Air Act Resources document emphasizes **spill prevention, control, and containment (SPCC)** plans as a critical component of data center operations ^10. These plans require:  
- **Secondary containment berms** around fuel storage tanks.  
- **Regular inspections** of fuel transfer systems.  
- **Emergency response protocols** for spills, including rapid cleanup and notification of regulatory agencies.  

The 2026 Equinix spill in New Jersey, where thousands of gallons of diesel entered Anderson Creek (a tributary of the Hackensack River), underscores the environmental consequences of inadequate containment ^9. In the Trent River watershed, similar risks would apply to the proposed sites, particularly if fuel storage or generator systems are not properly managed. Local authorities in Hastings County would need to enforce SPCC requirements to prevent contamination of the Trent River or Bay of Quinte.

### Case Studies: U.S. and International Examples  

#### 1. **U.S. EPA and the 2026 Equinix Spill**  

The 2026 diesel spill at an Equinix data center in Secaucus, New Jersey, serves as a cautionary tale for data center operators and regulators. The incident, which contaminated Anderson Creek, prompted the New Jersey Department of Environmental Protection to deploy cleanup crews and highlight the need for **robust containment systems** ^9. The EPA’s subsequent guidance on SPCC plans and generator emissions standards (e.g., Method 202 for condensable particulate matter) reflects a proactive approach to mitigating such risks ^7.  

#### 2. **European Union: Stricter Emission Standards**  

While the provided sources focus on the U.S., the EU has implemented stringent regulations for data centers, including:  
- **EU Emissions Trading System (ETS)**: Requires data centers to purchase carbon credits for emissions exceeding thresholds.  
- **Waste Electrical and Electronic Equipment (WEEE) Directive**: Mandates proper disposal of hazardous materials used in data center operations.  

These measures, though not detailed in the provided sources, align with the U.S. emphasis on **preventing environmental harm** through regulation. In Quinte West, similar frameworks could be adopted to ensure data centers adhere to provincial environmental standards.  

#### 3. **China: Rapid Expansion and Regulatory Challenges**  

China’s rapid data center growth has led to localized pollution issues, prompting the government to enforce **national emission standards** and **energy efficiency mandates**. For example, the **National Development and Reform Commission (NDRC)** has mandated that new data centers meet specific power usage effectiveness (PUE) ratios. While not covered in the provided sources, this approach mirrors the U.S. focus on **preventing overuse of resources** and **minimizing environmental impact**.

### Technical Breakdown: Key Measures for Data Center Safety  

#### 1. **Backup Power Systems and Emissions Control**  

Data centers rely on **backup generators** (e.g., diesel engines) during power outages. However, these systems emit pollutants such as nitrogen oxides (NOx) and particulate matter (PM). The EPA’s **National Emission Standards for Hazardous Air Pollutants (NESHAP)** for stationary engines require data centers to install **control technologies** (e.g., catalytic converters) to reduce emissions ^11.  

In Quinte West, the proposed data centers would need to comply with similar standards, ensuring that backup generators meet provincial and federal emission limits.  

#### 2. **Spill Containment and Waterway Protection**  

Fuel spills from data centers can contaminate waterways, as seen in the Equinix incident. The EPA’s **SPCC rule** mandates that facilities storing more than 1,000 gallons of fuel implement **secondary containment** and **emergency response plans** ^10.  

For the Trent River/Bay of Quinte watershed, this would mean:  
- **Secondary berms** around fuel tanks at 7 Riverside Drive and 920 Trenton Frankford Road.  
- **Regular inspections** of fuel lines and storage areas to prevent leaks.  
- **Collaboration with local agencies** (e.g., Hastings County) to ensure compliance with the **Canadian Environmental Protection Act (CEPA)**.  

#### 3. **Air Quality Modeling and Permitting**  

The EPA’s **Guideline on Air Quality Models (Appendix W)** provides tools for assessing the impact of data center emissions on local air quality ^5. These models help regulators determine whether a facility’s emissions would exceed **National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS)**.  

In Quinte West, similar modeling could be used to evaluate the potential impact of the proposed data centers on air quality in the Trent River watershed. This would involve:  
- **Emission inventories** for backup generators and cooling systems.  
- **Dispersion modeling** to predict pollutant concentrations near sensitive areas (e.g., residential zones, waterways).

### Conclusion and Recommendations for Quinte West and Hastings County  

The global response to data center accidents has centered on **regulatory frameworks**, **technological safeguards**, and **emergency preparedness**. The U.S. EPA’s Clean Air Act Resources and related studies provide a blueprint for managing risks, particularly in regions with vulnerable ecosystems like the Trent River/Bay of Quinte watershed.  

For the proposed data centers at 7 Riverside Drive and 920 Trenton Frankford Road, the following measures are recommended:  
1. **Adopt SPCC plans** with secondary containment berms to prevent fuel spills into the Trent River.  
2. **Comply with provincial and federal emission standards** for backup generators, mirroring U.S. NSR and NESHAP requirements.  
3. **Integrate air quality modeling** to assess the impact of emissions on local communities and the environment.  
4. **Collaborate with Hastings County and ECCC** to ensure adherence to CEPA and other environmental regulations.  

By learning from global best practices and applying them to the unique context of Quinte West, Hastings County can ensure that data center development supports economic growth without compromising environmental and public health.
