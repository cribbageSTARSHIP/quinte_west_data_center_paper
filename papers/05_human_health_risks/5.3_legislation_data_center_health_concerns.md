# How Governments in Canada and Worldwide Legislate Data Center Health Concerns  

## Introduction  

Data centers, as critical infrastructure for the digital economy, have grown in scale and energy demand, raising significant environmental and public health concerns. Governments globally, including Canada, have increasingly legislated to mitigate the adverse effects of data center operations, particularly on air quality, water resources, and human health. This paper examines the legislative frameworks, regulatory mechanisms, and technical standards employed by Canadian and international governments to address data center health concerns. Drawing on authoritative sources, including U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) materials, health impact studies, and environmental regulations, the analysis explores how policies are structured to balance technological advancement with public health protection. Special attention is given to the implications for the City of Quinte West, Hastings County, and the Trent River/Bay of Quinte watershed, particularly for proposed data center sites at 7 Riverside Drive (former Saputo Dairy) and 920 Trenton Frankford Road (former Sonoco Paper Mill).

## Legislative Frameworks for Data Center Health Concerns  

### **United States: Clean Air Act and EPA Regulations**  

In the United States, the Clean Air Act (CAA) serves as the cornerstone of air quality regulation, with specific provisions targeting stationary sources, including data centers and their backup power systems. The CAA mandates New Source Review (NSR) permitting, which requires facilities to obtain permits before constructing or modifying sources of air pollution. For data centers, this includes backup generators, which emit pollutants such as nitrogen oxides (NOₓ), particulate matter (PM), and volatile organic compounds (VOCs) ^2.  

The EPA has issued detailed guidance on air permitting for data centers, emphasizing compliance with National Emission Standards for Hazardous Air Pollutants (NESHAP) and New Source Performance Standards (NSPS) ^7. For example, the EPA’s “Clean Air Act Resources for Data Centers” document outlines how data centers must demonstrate compliance with CAA air quality standards, including the use of modeling tools like the COBRA model to estimate pollutant dispersion and health impacts ^10. These requirements are particularly relevant for facilities relying on stationary engines for backup power, which are subject to stringent emission limits under the NSPS for Stationary Spark Ignition Internal Combustion Engines ^8.  

### **Canada: Federal and Provincial Regulations**  

While the provided sources focus on U.S. legislation, Canadian regulatory frameworks for data centers are informed by similar principles, albeit with distinct legal and environmental priorities. At the federal level, Canada’s *Canadian Environmental Protection Act* (CEPA) and *Federal Sustainable Development Strategy* (FSDS) establish guidelines for managing air and water pollution from industrial facilities, including data centers ^12. Provincially, Ontario’s *Environmental Protection Act* (EPA) and *Ontario Regulation 347/03* under the *Air Quality Management System* (AQMS) impose permitting requirements for facilities emitting hazardous air pollutants.  

For example, data centers in Ontario must comply with the *Ontario Air Quality Standards* (OAQS), which set limits for PM₂.₅, NO₂, and other pollutants. The province also mandates the use of Best Available Technologies (BAT) to minimize emissions, a principle mirrored in U.S. NSR permitting ^12. However, unlike the U.S., Canada lacks a centralized federal program akin to the EPA’s CAA resources, relying instead on provincial enforcement and collaboration with industry stakeholders.  

### **Global Perspectives: EU, China, and Other Jurisdictions**  

Globally, the European Union (EU) has implemented stringent regulations through the *Industrial Emissions Directive* (IED), which requires data centers and other industrial facilities to obtain permits under the *Integrated Pollution Prevention and Control* (IPPC) regime. The IED mandates the use of BAT and sets emission limits for NOₓ, PM, and VOCs, similar to U.S. NSPS ^12.  

In China, the *Air Pollution Prevention and Control Law* (APPC Law) and *National Emission Standards for Air Pollutants* (NESAP) regulate data center emissions, with a focus on reducing coal-based power generation and promoting renewable energy integration. The Chinese government has also emphasized the use of carbon capture and storage (CCS) technologies for large-scale facilities, though these measures are less common in North America ^12.

## Technical Regulations and Compliance Mechanisms  

### **Emission Standards for Backup Generators**  

Data centers often rely on diesel or natural gas generators for backup power, which are significant sources of air pollution. In the U.S., the EPA’s NSPS for Stationary Spark Ignition Internal Combustion Engines (40 CFR Part 60) sets limits for NOₓ and PM emissions, requiring facilities to install pollution control technologies such as selective catalytic reduction (SCR) or diesel particulate filters (DPFs) ^8. These standards are critical for facilities like those proposed in Quinte West, where proximity to the Trent River watershed could exacerbate local air quality issues.  

For example, the EPA’s guidance on *Potential to Emit (PTE)* for emergency generators clarifies that facilities must account for 500 hours of annual generator operation when calculating emission limits, ensuring that even intermittent backup power systems comply with CAA requirements ^8. This is particularly relevant for the 920 Trenton Frankford Road site, where a former paper mill’s infrastructure may require retrofitting to meet modern emission standards.  

### **Water and Waste Management Regulations**  

Beyond air quality, data centers generate significant water usage for cooling systems and may produce hazardous waste from chemical processes. In the U.S., the *Clean Water Act* (CWA) and *Resource Conservation and Recovery Act* (RCRA) regulate discharges into water bodies and hazardous waste management, respectively. For instance, the EPA’s *National Pollutant Discharge Elimination System* (NPDES) permits require data centers to monitor and report discharges of cooling water, which could impact the Trent River’s ecology ^12.  

In Canada, the *Fisheries Act* and *Canadian Environmental Protection Act* (CEPA) impose similar restrictions, prohibiting the discharge of pollutants that could harm aquatic ecosystems. For the Trent River watershed, which is a critical habitat for fish and wildlife, compliance with these regulations would be essential to prevent contamination from data center operations.  

### **Health Impact Assessments and Public Participation**  

Health concerns associated with data centers are increasingly addressed through mandatory health impact assessments (HIAs). The EPA’s COBRA model, used in studies estimating asthma cases and mortality linked to data center emissions, highlights the need for HIAs in permitting processes ^10. For example, a 2028 projection under a high-growth scenario suggests that U.S. data centers could contribute to 600,000 asthma symptom cases and 1,300 deaths annually, with public health costs exceeding $20 billion ^10. While these figures are U.S.-specific, they underscore the potential risks for communities near proposed data centers, such as Quinte West.  

In Canada, HIAs are not universally mandated but are increasingly encouraged under provincial environmental assessments. For instance, the City of Quinte West’s proposed data center sites would need to undergo rigorous HIAs to evaluate risks to the local population, particularly given the proximity to the Trent River watershed and Hastings County’s rural demographics.

## Case Study: Quinte West, Hastings County, and the Trent River Watershed  

### **Site-Specific Regulatory Challenges**  

The proposed data center sites at 7 Riverside Drive (former Saputo Dairy) and 920 Trenton Frankford Road (former Sonoco Paper Mill) present unique regulatory challenges due to their environmental sensitivity. The Trent River, a key tributary of the Bay of Quinte, is designated as a *Canadian Heritage River* and is home to diverse aquatic species, including the endangered *Lake Sturgeon* ^12. Any data center operations in the area would need to comply with strict water quality standards under the *Fisheries Act* and *Ontario’s Clean Water Act*.  

For example, the use of cooling towers at the 7 Riverside Drive site could lead to thermal pollution, affecting fish populations in the Trent River. To mitigate this, the facility would need to implement closed-loop cooling systems or use alternative water sources, as required under the CWA’s NPDES permitting ^12. Similarly, the 920 Trenton Frankford Road site, located near a former paper mill, may face challenges related to soil contamination and hazardous waste management, requiring compliance with RCRA and CEPA regulations.  

### **Community and Health Implications**  

The health risks associated with data centers in Quinte West would depend on their energy sources and emission control technologies. If the facilities rely on diesel generators, they could contribute to local air pollution, increasing the risk of respiratory illnesses in Hastings County. The EPA’s COBRA model suggests that even moderate increases in NOₓ and PM emissions could lead to significant public health costs, particularly in rural areas with limited healthcare access ^10.  

To address these concerns, the City of Quinte West would need to enforce strict permitting requirements, including the use of low-emission generators and real-time air quality monitoring. Collaboration with the *Bay of Quinte Conservation Authority* would also be essential to ensure that data center operations do not compromise the Trent River’s ecological integrity.

## Conclusion  

The regulation of data center health concerns is a complex interplay of federal, provincial, and international policies, with the U.S. Clean Air Act and EPA guidelines serving as a global benchmark. While Canada’s regulatory framework shares similarities with the U.S., it emphasizes provincial oversight and environmental protection for sensitive ecosystems like the Trent River watershed. For proposed sites in Quinte West and Hastings County, compliance with air quality, water, and health standards will be critical to ensuring sustainable development. As data center demand grows, governments must continue refining regulations to balance technological innovation with public health and environmental protection.
